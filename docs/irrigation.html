Lens one

Irrigation, and the shape nobody expects

The usual claim is that irrigation drives monoculture. What the data show is a rise, a turn, and then a fall, with the source of the water carrying information the quantity does not.

Irrigation is the variable most often held responsible for the narrowing of Indian cropping. The mechanism is plausible and widely repeated: assured water makes the high-value cereal package viable, the cereal package pays, and everything else is squeezed out of the rotation. If that were the whole story, diversity would fall steadily as irrigation rises.

It does not. The relationship turns.

Irrigation is measured here from village records that report irrigated and unirrigated land separately, as irrigated land over the sum of the two. The measure is closed by construction and needs no external denominator, and it reproduces published state irrigation shares closely enough to be trusted at district level.

Each point is a district. The line is a fitted quadratic, with the vertical rule marking where it turns.

The rising arm is easy to rationalise: water relaxes the constraint that forces rainfed districts into a small set of hardy crops. The falling arm is the part that needs an explanation, and the market lens takes it up.

The northwestern belt and the Gangetic plain run near-fully irrigated. Reading this against the diversity map on the overview is the whole of the relationship in two pictures.

Binning districts into deciles of irrigation, rather than fitting a curve through them, gives the same shape without imposing a functional form. The pattern holds on the count of crops grown as well as on the effective count, which matters because the two can move independently.

Interrogation

Does the shape survive being attacked?

A quadratic fitted to a cross-section will often produce a turning point whether or not one exists, so the curvature was re-estimated across a series of deliberately awkward variations. Eight of them change the sample: dropping fixed effects, swapping in an entirely independent irrigation measure built from the 2011 Village Directory rather than Mission Antyodaya, removing the districts that share a pre-2011 parent and therefore carry duplicated covariates, weighting by cropped area, and so on. Six more change the index rather than the sample.

Standard errors throughout are clustered on the pre-2011 parent district, because a number of present-day districts were carved out of a common ancestor and inherit identical rural structure.

Figure 4

Curvature across specifications and indices

Coefficient on irrigation squared. A negative value means the curve bends downward, which is what a turning point requires.

Hollow markers would indicate a coefficient not distinguishable from zero at conventional levels. There are none across fourteen variations.

One qualification belongs with that figure. The turning point is not a fixed quantity. It moves depending on which index is used and whether state fixed effects are included, so it is better quoted as a range somewhere between a third and a half of sown area than as a point estimate. What does not move is the sign of the curvature, which is negative everywhere.

The shape also appears on the plain count of crops, on both effective counts, and on evenness taken by itself. That last one is worth pausing on, because it means irrigation is doing two things at once rather than one: it is associated with how many crops a district grows, and separately with how evenly the land is divided between them.

What this is not Nothing here identifies an effect of irrigation. Canal commands and tubewell belts were developed where soils, aquifers, terrain and politics already favoured particular agriculture. The curve describes how the cross-section is arranged, and a district that installed irrigation tomorrow would not travel along it.

Composition

Where the water comes from

Irrigation is usually treated as a quantity. It is also a technology, and the technologies behave differently. A canal command delivers water on a schedule set by an authority, in a rotation that suits the crop the system was designed around. A tubewell delivers water when the farmer decides to switch on a pump. Surface sources sit between the two and are the least reliable.

Mission Antyodaya records the dominant irrigation source for each village, and the four categories partition cleanly. Groundwater is the most common by a wide margin.

Figure 5

Dominant irrigation source across villages

Mean share of a district's villages reporting each source as dominant.

Entering the source shares alongside irrigation and its square, with groundwater as the omitted category, separates two effects that a single irrigation variable cannot. The comparison is therefore between a district irrigated mainly by canal or by surface water and an otherwise similar district irrigated mainly by tubewell.

Figure 6

Source composition, holding the level of irrigation constant

Coefficients relative to groundwater, with state fixed effects and errors clustered on the parent district.

canal, against groundwater surface water, against groundwater

Hollow markers are not distinguishable from zero at the five percent level. Outcomes are in different units, so compare within a row and not across rows.

The pattern that emerges is not the one the canal-command hypothesis predicts. Canal dependence is associated with a shorter crop list, cutting the count of crops grown, but it leaves the effective count largely alone and it goes with a somewhat larger pulse share. Surface-water dependence behaves quite differently: it is associated with lower diversity on the effective measures and with a substantially larger cereal share.

Read together, the simple claim that canals force monoculture does not survive. Canals narrow the range of crops without concentrating land onto one of them. Surface-water reliance, which is the least controllable of the three, is what goes with cereal concentration. That is consistent with a risk story rather than a scheduling story, though this design cannot adjudicate between them.

Context

Irrigation against everything else

Irrigation is one of several district characteristics that could be lined up against diversity. Placing them on a common footing, each entered on its own and then again with irrigation, holding size and state fixed effects, gives a sense of what else is in play before the market lens takes over.

Adjusted coefficients. Variables are on different scales, so the ordering matters more than the magnitudes.

Mean holding size sits at essentially zero throughout. The inverse relationship between farm size and diversity, which is well established in the household literature, does not appear at district level here. That is at least as likely to be a comment on the proxy as on the relationship.
