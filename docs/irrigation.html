Irrigation

How irrigation lines up with crop diversity

Across 606 districts, the effective number of crops is highest where a moderate share of sown area is irrigated and lower toward both ends of the range.

01 · The curve

Compared within their own state, districts with about 28 percent of sown area irrigated have the highest effective number of crops.

Each dot is one of 606 districts, shaded by its irrigation group. The curve is a quadratic fit with a 95 percent band. Within states, it is drawn for a district at the average state baseline, the comparison that sets each district only against districts in its own state. Irrigation from Mission Antyodaya 2019–20 via SHRUG 2.1. Crops from district area statistics, 1997–98 to 2019–20. Hover or tap a dot for the district.

Each dot is a district. Irrigation is the share of its net sown area that village records list as irrigated in the 2019–20 Mission Antyodaya survey. The diversity measures come from district crop area statistics. We compute each one within a year and average it over the years a district is observed, a median of 23 years between 1997–98 and 2019–20, and leave out districts with fewer than 10 years of records.

The count of crops is the number of crops a district grows in a year, 21.2 in the average district. The effective number of crops weighs each crop by its share of land. It equals the number of equally sized crops that would give the same spread of land. It averages 4.9 because in the typical district the largest crop alone holds close to half the area. It is the exponential of the Shannon index of crop area shares.

The line is a quadratic fit, which lets the relationship rise and then fall once. When all districts are compared together, the fit peaks at 51 percent irrigated and explains 6 percent of the variation between districts. Adding the state baseline compares each district only with districts in its own state. That removes everything a state's districts share, such as policy, rainfall and the way records are kept. The peak then sits at 28 percent irrigated. Explained variation rises to 53 percent, most of it from the state baseline itself. On the count of crops the within-state curve peaks later, at 46 percent irrigated.

These are comparisons between districts at one point in time. Canal commands and tubewell belts were built where soils, aquifers and terrain already favoured some kinds of farming. A district that added irrigation would not necessarily move along this curve.

02 · Ten groups of districts

The effective number of crops peaks at 5.9 in the fourth tenth of districts by irrigation and falls to 3.7 in the most irrigated tenth.

District means for each tenth of the irrigated share, about 60 districts per group, placed at the group's mean irrigated share. Whiskers span one standard error of the group mean. Hover or tap a group for its values.

Here districts are sorted by irrigated share into ten groups of about 60 each, with no curve fitted. The least irrigated tenth has 16.5 percent of sown area irrigated on average and the most irrigated tenth 92.2 percent.

The effective number of crops rises from 4.11 in the first group to 5.86 in the fourth, where irrigation averages 44 percent, and falls to 3.71 in the tenth. That is about 2.2 below the peak. The fifth group dips and the sixth recovers. Each group holds about 60 districts, so a single group mean can move by chance.

The count of crops peaks two groups later, at 24.3 in the eighth group, and falls to 18.0 in the tenth. Evenness is the effective number of crops divided by the count. It is highest in the fourth group at 0.286 and lowest in the eighth at 0.213, the group with the longest list of crops.

03 · Where

Districts in Punjab, Haryana and Uttar Pradesh irrigate more than four-fifths of their sown area on average.

Irrigated land over irrigated plus unirrigated land, Mission Antyodaya 2019–20 via SHRUG 2.1. White lines are state borders. Grey districts are outside the 606 used here. Hover or tap a district for its values, or a group in the key to pick out its districts.

The northwest and the Gangetic plain irrigate most of their sown area. Punjab averages 92 percent across its districts, Haryana 86 and Uttar Pradesh 84. The least irrigated districts are in Kerala, the Himalayan states, the northeast and Chhattisgarh. Kerala averages 23 percent and Himachal Pradesh 25.

Splitting districts at 40 and 60 percent irrigated gives three groups. The 168 districts below 40 percent have an average effective number of crops of 4.67, the 196 between 40 and 60 percent average 5.55, and the 242 above 60 percent average 4.61. The count of crops differs little across the groups, from 20.5 to 21.5. With irrigation measured from the 2011 census village directory instead, the middle group again has the highest effective number, 5.63.

The irrigated share is irrigated land divided by irrigated plus unirrigated land, both from the same village records, which makes it a share of net sown area. The 2011 census village directory gives a second measure of irrigation, and across districts the two agree at a rank correlation of 0.81.

04 · What the land is used for

Cereals take 79 percent of gross cropped area in the most irrigated tenth of districts, against 57 percent in the seventh.

A crop group's area over gross cropped area, averaged over the years each district is observed. Whiskers span one standard error of the group mean. The shaded band marks the three most irrigated groups, where the effective number of crops falls.

An area share is a crop group's area divided by the district's gross cropped area, which counts a field twice when it carries two crops in a year. Cereals hold the largest share in every group of districts.

Cereal share falls from 0.718 in the least irrigated tenth to 0.574 in the seventh and then climbs to 0.772 in the ninth and 0.793 in the tenth. Pulses and oilseeds lose ground over the same top groups. Pulse share drops from 0.137 in the eighth tenth to 0.023 in the tenth, and oilseed share from 0.197 in the seventh to 0.041.

The effective number of crops falls when more of the land goes to fewer crops. Across the three most irrigated groups it declines from 4.96 to 3.71, over the same groups where cereal share rises.

05 · How firm the bend is

The fitted curve bends downward in all fourteen versions of the estimate.

effective number of crops other measures of diversity headline

The turning point is the irrigated share at which the fitted curve peaks. Filled dots have p < 0.05 on the squared term, and all fourteen are filled. Every version except the first includes the state baseline. Districts: 606 unless shown.

A quadratic fitted across districts can produce a peak even where the true relationship only levels off. The table below checks the bend in fourteen versions of the comparison. The squared term on irrigation measures the bend, and a negative value means the fitted line turns down inside the range.

Eight versions keep the effective number of crops. The first is the headline curve within states, and the other seven change the comparison. One drops the state baseline. One measures irrigation from the 2011 census village directory (573 districts). One leaves out the 64 districts whose 2011 village records also cover another district in the crop records, mostly a newer district split from them (542). Another leaves out districts where the 2011 and 2019–20 records name different main irrigation sources (530). One keeps only districts with at least 22 years of records (506). The last two weight districts by cropped area and weight down the districts that share 2011 records.

Six versions change the measure of diversity. They use the count of crops, the number of main crops, evenness, the raw Shannon and Simpson indices, and a composite index kept for comparison.

The squared term is negative in all fourteen. Evenness is the weakest at p = 0.013, and the other thirteen are below 0.001. The peak moves with the measure. Across the eight versions on the effective number of crops it runs from 24 to 51 percent irrigated, with a median of 27, and only the version without the state baseline puts it above 36. Across all fourteen it runs from 18 percent, for evenness, to 51 percent.

Evenness has the count of crops divided out, so it measures how evenly land is split across crops whatever their number. It also bends downward with irrigation.

06 · Where the water comes from

Groundwater is the main irrigation source in half of a district's villages on average.

Mean share of a district's villages naming each source as main, each village counted once. Mission Antyodaya 2019–20.

Mission Antyodaya records one main irrigation source for each village. Across districts, groundwater is the main source in 50.5 percent of villages on average, canal in 17.2 percent and surface water in 11.3 percent. Surface water here covers tanks, ponds and lifts from rivers. The remaining 21.1 percent are recorded as other, a catch-all class in the survey that names no technology.

A village share counts each village once whatever its size, so a district of many small villages weighs the same as one of a few large ones. A village that takes half its water from a canal and half from a tubewell counts wholly toward the source it names as main.

Groundwater rises steadily with irrigation, from 23 percent of villages in the least irrigated tenth of districts to 78 percent in the most irrigated. Surface water and other move the opposite way, and both are highest where irrigation is lowest. Canal shares are largest in the upper middle of the range, 23 percent in the seventh tenth.

The sources supply water on different terms. A canal runs on a schedule set by an irrigation authority, usually built around the crop the system was designed for. With a tubewell the farmer decides when to run the pump, within the limits of the pump and the water table. Tanks and ponds hold what that season's rain puts in them.

07 · Source at the same level of irrigation

At the same irrigated share, districts where more villages rely on surface water have a lower effective number of crops and more land in cereals.

canal surface water p ≥ 0.05

Coefficients against groundwater and other sources, with the irrigation curve, log mean holding size, Scheduled Tribe population share and state baseline held constant. Whiskers are 95 percent intervals. 606 districts.

To separate the source of water from the amount, we add the village shares relying on canals and on surface water to the within-state curve, along with log mean holding size and the Scheduled Tribe population share. Villages on groundwater and on other sources form the comparison. Each estimate compares districts at the same irrigated share in the same state.

Surface water lines up with lower diversity. A district where every village relies on surface water has an effective number of crops 2.03 lower than one where none does (p = 0.026), and a cereal share 0.30 higher (p = 0.0001). Surface water is the main source in 11 percent of villages on average. A difference of 10 percentage points of villages goes with an effective number of crops about 0.2 lower and a cereal share 0.03 higher. Over the full range there are 1.66 fewer main crops (p = 0.019). Pulse and oilseed shares are lower too, by 0.10 and 0.16. The cereal difference of 0.30 is larger than the whole spread of cereal share across the ten irrigation groups, which runs from 0.574 to 0.793.

Canal dependence lines up with a shorter list of crops. A district where every village relies on a canal grows 2.59 fewer crops (p = 0.003), with no clear difference in the effective number of crops (−0.31, p = 0.42). Since the effective number weighs crops by land, the crops missing from canal districts are most likely ones that held little area. Pulse share is 0.073 higher (p = 0.0005) and oilseed share 0.097 lower (p = 0.002).

Nothing here measures how reliable each source is. These estimates cannot tell an effect of uncertain supply apart from an effect of canal scheduling.

08 · Other district characteristics

Producer organisations and cropping intensity are the two district characteristics still linked to the effective number of crops once irrigation, holding size and the state baseline are held constant.

on its own adjusted, p < 0.05 adjusted, p ≥ 0.05

Adjusted adds the irrigation curve, log mean holding size and the state baseline. 606 districts (landless share 592). Per standard deviation values use the spread across the 606 districts.

We compare each characteristic with the effective number of crops twice. The first comparison is on its own. The second adds the irrigation curve, log mean holding size and the state baseline, and shows which characteristics still line up with diversity once irrigation, farm size and state are held constant.

Two keep a clear association. Producer organisations are farmer groups that sell produce and buy inputs jointly. A district where every village has one has an effective number of crops 1.91 higher than one where none does (p = 0.026), about 0.37 for one standard deviation of the share. Cropping intensity is gross cropped area over net sown area. It carries +2.27 per unit (p < 0.0001), about 0.55 for one standard deviation.

Food storage warehouses, regular markets, canal share and the cultivator share of agricultural workers each have a clear association on their own that fades with the adjustment. Mean holding size is +0.34 on its own (p = 0.006) and +0.02 once irrigation and state are held constant (p = 0.88). A district mean says little about how holdings are spread within a district, so this is a weak test of farm size.

The irrigated share on its own has a coefficient of −0.75, because a straight line drawn through a rise and a fall comes out slightly negative. With the squared term included, the linear term is +3.90.

How this was estimated

Of 725 districts with crop area records, 606 in 30 states are used. 101 are left out for having fewer than 10 years of records. The other 18 have no usable village record, either because the survey reaches under a quarter of their villages or because no Mission Antyodaya record matches them, mostly in Tripura, Puducherry and Jammu and Kashmir. The districts used have a median of 23 years of records between 1997–98 and 2019–20.

The count of crops, the effective number of crops (the exponential of the Shannon index), the number of main crops (the inverse Simpson index, one over the sum of squared land shares, which gives the largest crops more weight) and evenness (the effective number divided by the count) are each computed within a year and then averaged across years. Their means across the 606 districts are 21.2, 4.9, 3.5 and 0.254. Area shares are over gross cropped area.

The irrigated share is irrigated land over irrigated plus unirrigated land from Mission Antyodaya 2019–20, a share of net sown area. Its state averages run above published state irrigation figures in Maharashtra, Jharkhand and Assam. Leaving those three states out gives a squared term of −6.14 (523 districts, p < 0.0001), with the peak at 21 percent irrigated. Against the 2011 census village directory the district measure agrees at a rank correlation of 0.81.

The model is D = b0 + b1 · irr + b2 · irr², with the state baseline in every version but one. The turning point is −b1 / (2 · b2). The within-state curve in the first chart takes its intercept from the state baselines averaged over the 606 districts, so its mean across the districts equals the sample mean of 4.93. R² is 0.065 without the state baseline and 0.530 with it.

Standard errors allow for uneven scatter and group districts by the 2011 district whose village records they carry. For 64 districts that 2011 district also covers another district in the crop records, mostly a newer district split from it that is outside the sample. Only Sultanpur and Amethi are both in the sample, so the 606 districts form 605 groups and the grouping changes little. Leaving the 64 out gives a squared term of −6.38 (542 districts). Weighting each district by one over the number of crop-record districts that share its 2011 records gives −6.61. The headline is −6.88.

The three irrigation groups are cut at 40 and 60 percent irrigated. The source comparison enters the canal and surface village shares together with the irrigation curve, log mean holding size, the Scheduled Tribe population share and the state baseline. The characteristic comparison enters each characteristic first on its own and then with the irrigation curve, log mean holding size and the state baseline, leaving out the holding term when the characteristic is holding size.

The village source record is a single 2019–20 cross-section set against two decades of cropping. Cropping intensity agrees only moderately with published state figures. Every coefficient here is a comparison between districts at one point in time.
