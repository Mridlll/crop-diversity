Irrigation

Districts irrigated early grow fewer crops today

Within each state, the districts that were already heavily irrigated in 1991 grow about two fewer crops in effective terms today than the least irrigated, and most of the fall comes above about half of sown area irrigated. Where irrigation spread after 1991, no matching fall shows up, although the records follow that spread too loosely to rule one out.

01 · The shape

Within a state, the effective number of crops is roughly flat across the less irrigated districts and falls once irrigation covers more than about half of sown area.

Difference from the least irrigated group of districts in the same state, 1998–99 to 2019–20. Each step spans its group's irrigation range, whiskers are 95 percent intervals and filled dots have p < 0.05. Hover or tap for values.

Against the least irrigated districts of its own state, a district in the most irrigated quintile, above 77 percent of sown area, grows 2.14 fewer crops in effective terms (p = 0.013), about two-fifths of the average district's 4.9. Up to 60 percent irrigated the gaps are small and not statistically significant.

The bend fits best at 43 percent, yet anywhere from about 30 to 70 percent fits almost as well, and a bent line beats a steady decline only weakly (p = 0.07). Since canal commands and tubewell belts went where soils and aquifers already favoured some kinds of farming, these figures describe how districts differ, and a district that adds irrigation need not follow them.

02 · Recent years

The fall in heavily irrigated districts holds in every recent set of years, up to 2021–22.

Difference from the least irrigated quintile of the same state, with 95 percent intervals. Filled dots have p < 0.05. Hover or tap a quintile for values.

In 2017–18 to 2021–22 the most irrigated quintile grows 1.98 fewer effective crops than the least irrigated quintile of its state, and in the last two years alone 2.67 fewer. The second and third quintiles stay within a third of a crop of the least irrigated.

03 · Irrigation in 1991

Districts already heavily irrigated in 1991, before the crop record begins, grow fewer crops in effective terms today.

Share of sown area irrigated in the 1991 census village records. Difference from the least irrigated quintile of the same state, at the same elevation and ruggedness, with 95 percent intervals. Filled dots have p < 0.05.

Irrigation covered 34 percent of sown area in the average district in 1991, seven years before the crop record begins, and the districts that irrigated most then still irrigate most today.

Districts that already irrigated more than about 63 percent of their sown area in 1991 grow 1.78 fewer effective crops in 2017–18 to 2021–22 than the least irrigated quintile of their state (p = 0.002). Below the top quintile the gaps are small and not statistically significant, and the drier districts of 1991 show no rise to match the later decline.

04 · Where irrigation spread

Districts where irrigation spread faster between 1991 and 2011 did not end up with a different effective number of crops from others in their state.

Change in the effective number of crops from 1998–99 to 2002–03, within state, for 10 more percentage points of sown area irrigated gained between the 1991 and 2011 censuses. Whiskers are 95 percent intervals.

Irrigation spread to another 12 percent of sown area in the average district between the 1991 and 2011 censuses, mostly from wells and tubewells. A district that gained 10 points more than others in its state saw its effective number of crops change by only +0.02 to +0.06, none of it statistically significant.

The census and the yearly land-use figures disagree so widely about how far irrigation spread in each district that a modest change in either direction could hide inside these estimates.

05 · Inside each district

A district's effective number of crops barely moves as its own irrigated share rises, although the yearly irrigation figures are too noisy to rule out a real fall.

Change in the effective number of crops for 10 more percentage points of sown area irrigated, with 95 percent intervals. The bottom estimate allows for noise in the yearly irrigation figures.

Across districts of the same state, once irrigation passes half of sown area, every 10 more points goes with about 0.6 fewer effective crops. Inside a district the line goes flat, at between −0.06 and +0.02 on a mean of 4.9.

That flat line proves less than it seems, because the yearly irrigation figures and the census agree so poorly on how much each district's irrigation grew that most of the change in the yearly record is noise. Allowing for it widens the range to between −0.52 and +0.13 per 10 points, which only just excludes the fall seen across districts, and a few large changes carry much of that noise.

06 · Deciles of irrigation

Raw averages put the most effective crops in the fourth decile of irrigation, although within state none of the first seven deciles differs significantly from the least irrigated.

District means by decile of irrigated share across all districts, with whiskers of one standard error. Hover or tap a decile for its values.

Across the whole country the effective number of crops rises from 4.13 in the driest decile to 5.89 in the fourth, where irrigation averages 44 percent, then drops to 3.69 in the most irrigated. Those raw averages mix irrigation with everything else that separates one state from another, and within state the fourth decile's lead is not statistically significant.

The count of crops peaks two deciles later, at 24.5 in the eighth, even though that decile spreads its land least evenly.

07 · Where

Districts in Punjab, Haryana and Uttar Pradesh irrigate more than four-fifths of their sown area on average.

Share of net sown area irrigated, 2019–20 village records. White lines are state borders and grey districts have no usable record. Hover or tap a district for its values, or a group in the key to pick out its districts.

Punjab averages 92 percent across its districts, Haryana 86 and Uttar Pradesh 84, while Kerala averages 23 percent and Himachal Pradesh 25.

The most irrigated districts grow about as many crops as anywhere else, yet average 4.62 on the effective number. Because the least irrigated group gathers Kerala, the hills and the northeast, its own average of 4.69 says as much about those regions as about water.

08 · What the land is used for

Cereals take 79 percent of gross cropped area in the most irrigated decile of districts, against 58 percent in the seventh.

A crop group's share of gross cropped area, with whiskers of one standard error. The shaded band marks the three most irrigated deciles.

Cereal share falls from 72 percent of cropped land in the driest decile to 58 percent in the seventh, then climbs to 79 percent in the most irrigated as pulses and oilseeds give way. Over the three most irrigated deciles the effective number of crops falls from 4.99 to 3.69, so the districts where cereals gain are also putting more of their land under fewer crops.

09 · The curve at high irrigation

The effective number of crops curves downward at high irrigation whichever irrigation record is used, and no single state carries it.

main estimate

The squared term measures how sharply the effective number of crops falls away at high irrigation. Filled dots have p < 0.05, and every estimate except the first compares districts within state.

Within state the squared term stays between −5.44 and −8.18 against −6.84 in the main estimate, and with any one state left out it stays between −7.27 and −4.61. The count of crops curves downward as well (p = 0.0001), whereas evenness curves least firmly and not significantly in recent years.

10 · Where the water comes from

Groundwater is the main irrigation source in half of a district's villages on average.

Mean share of a district's villages naming each source as their main one, 2019–20.

Groundwater rises from 23 percent of villages in the least irrigated decile of districts to 78 percent in the most irrigated, while tanks, ponds and unnamed sources are most common where irrigation is lowest. Canals peak in the upper middle of the range, at 23 percent of villages in the seventh decile.

A canal runs on a schedule set by an irrigation authority, usually built around the crop the system was designed for, whereas a farmer with a tubewell decides when to run the pump. Tanks and ponds hold only what that season's rain puts in them.

11 · Source at the same irrigated share

At the same irrigated share, districts where more villages rely on surface water have a lower effective number of crops and more land in cereals.

canal surface water p ≥ 0.05

Difference from a district where every village relies on groundwater or other sources, at the same irrigated share and within the same state. Whiskers are 95 percent intervals.

At the same irrigated share, a district in which every village relies on surface water has an effective number of crops 2.03 lower than one where none does (p = 0.029) and a cereal share 0.30 higher. Since surface water is the main source in only 11 percent of villages on average, 10 more points of villages is a more realistic step, and it goes with about 0.2 fewer effective crops.

Canal districts grow a shorter list, 2.61 fewer crops (p = 0.003), yet their effective number is barely lower, so the missing crops most likely held little land. Nothing records how reliable each source is, which leaves uncertain supply and canal scheduling impossible to tell apart.

12 · Canals, wells and tanks

The fall at high irrigation appears whether the water comes from canals or wells, and the data cannot tell the two apart.

Share of sown area irrigated from each source in the 2011 census, against the effective number of crops in 2009–10 to 2013–14, within state. Values are per within-state standard deviation, and filled dots have p < 0.05.

Within state, one standard deviation more sown area under canals goes with 0.27 fewer effective crops and one more under wells and tubewells with 0.25 fewer, and the two cannot be told apart.

Tank irrigation has the clearest link. One standard deviation of sown area under tanks and lakes, about 5 percentage points, goes with 0.48 fewer effective crops (p = 0.008), and it holds outside the four southern states where tanks are concentrated.

13 · Other district characteristics

Farmer collectives and cropping intensity are the two district characteristics still linked to the effective number of crops once irrigation and holding size are held constant.

on its own adjusted, p < 0.05 adjusted, p ≥ 0.05

Adjusted means within state, with irrigated share, its square and mean holding size held constant. Facilities are shares of villages, and values are per standard deviation of each characteristic.

A district where every village has a farmer collective, meaning a producer organisation or a primary agricultural credit society, scores 1.88 higher on the effective number of crops than one where none does (p = 0.029), about 0.36 for one standard deviation. Cropping intensity adds about 0.56 per standard deviation.

Food storage, regular markets, canal dependence and the cultivator share each line up with crop diversity on their own, yet fade within state once irrigation is held constant. Mean holding size falls from +0.35 to +0.02, partly because a district mean says little about how holdings are spread inside it.

How this was estimated

The comparisons across districts use 606 districts with at least 10 years of crop records and a usable village record. Irrigation is the share of net sown area that village records list as irrigated in the 2019–20 Mission Antyodaya survey.

The four diversity measures are each computed within a year and averaged over 1998–99 to 2019–20. Their district means are 21.4 for the count of crops, 4.9 for the effective number, 3.5 for the number of main crops and 0.250 for evenness, and area shares are over gross cropped area.

Quintiles and deciles. Districts are cut into quintiles or deciles of the irrigated share across the whole sample, and the effective number of crops is regressed on the group indicators with state fixed effects, so each figure is the gap to the least irrigated group among districts of the same state. In the view of every district, each dot is the district's effective number of crops less its state's fixed effect, and the dots in each quintile average exactly to that quintile's gap. Intervals use standard errors clustered by state with critical values from a t distribution with one fewer degree of freedom than the number of states. Every p-value in the first four charts and in the source chart comes from a wild cluster bootstrap by state (restricted, Webb weights, 9,999 draws), which is more cautious than the intervals drawn, so a whisker can just clear zero while p sits a little above 0.05.

Where the line bends. The two-piece line has a single change of slope, placed at half of sown area irrigated, with 56 percent of districts above it. Across bends from 20 to 80 percent, with state fixed effects, the best fit is at 43 percent; bends whose fit is not significantly worse (likelihood ratio, 95 percent) run from 36 to 65 percent, and a wild cluster bootstrap by state (Webb weights, 1,999 draws) puts the 95 percent interval at 29 to 72 percent. Half lies inside both, so the round figure is drawn. A bent line fits better than a straight one with p = 0.077 over 1998–99 to 2019–20 and p = 0.021 in 2017–18 to 2021–22, where the best bend is at 58 percent (largest Wald statistic over all bends, bootstrapped by state). With any one state left out the best bend stays between 42 and 60 percent. On the other irrigation records it sits lower, at 16 percent on the land-use statistics averaged over 1998–99 to 2019–20 and at 19 percent on the 2011 census, both of which run below the village records of 2019–20, so where the fall begins depends on which record measures irrigation. Among the other measures of diversity, the number of main crops at 0.5 percent bends at 57 percent, the count of crops at 38 percent and the number of main crops by inverse Simpson at 42 percent, none of them more precisely.

Recent years. Crop records extend to 2021–22. From 2020–21 a few crops are listed differently, so the windows reaching past 2019–20 leave out six minor crops (niger seed, coriander, garlic, turmeric, cashewnut and banana) in every year, together with state-years whose returns are unreliable: Manipur, Madhya Pradesh and Jammu and Kashmir from 2020–21, and Meghalaya, Nagaland, Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu in 2021–22. A district needs at least three of five years in a window and both years in 2020–21 and 2021–22. Removing the six crops lowers the count of crops by about two everywhere and barely moves any estimate.

Irrigation in 1991 and 2011. The 1991, 2001 and 2011 census village directories give irrigated area by source. Where a 1991 village leaves its total blank but reports irrigation by source, the larger of the two is used. The 1991 comparison keeps the 539 districts with a usable 1991 record and holds mean elevation and ruggedness constant. The long differences regress the change in the effective number of crops, from 1998–99 to 2002–03 to either 2009–10 to 2013–14 or 2015–16 to 2019–20, on the 1991 to 2011 change in irrigated share, with state fixed effects and terrain controls; the stable-area sample keeps districts whose mean gross cropped area stays within two-thirds and one and a half times its early level, and it gives the same answer. A smooth curve through the 539 districts of 1991 bends downward clearly in 2017–18 to 2021–22 (p = 0.010) but less so over the full period (p = 0.11), and less again (p = 0.19) when 1991 totals are taken as villages reported them.

Sources. The source comparison enters the 2011 shares of sown area irrigated by canals and by wells and tubewells, each with its square, and the tank and river shares, with elevation, ruggedness and state fixed effects, on 534 districts outside Maharashtra. Canal and well effects are read at the average district. Whether the curve differs with the source is read from a fit that enters irrigated share and its square, each interacted with groundwater's share of irrigated land.

Summed to the state, the village-record share ranks states almost exactly as the official land-use statistics do for 2017–18 to 2019–20 (rank correlation 0.91 across 21 states), although it runs well above them in Jharkhand, 0.45 against 0.18, and in Assam, 0.35 against 0.14. Maharashtra cannot be checked, because its land-use statistics carry no net irrigated area for those years. Leaving Maharashtra, Jharkhand and Assam out gives a squared term of −6.09 (p < 0.0001). Against the 2011 census village directory the district measure agrees at a rank correlation of 0.81.

The chart of the squared term fits D = b0 + b1 · irr + b2 · irr², with state fixed effects in every fit but one. R² is 0.067 across all districts together and 0.534 within state.

Unless stated otherwise, standard errors allow for uneven scatter and group districts by the 2011 district whose village records they carry. Leaving out districts whose 2011 records also cover another district gives a squared term of −6.38, and weighting each district by one over the number of districts sharing its 2011 records gives −6.60, against −6.84 in the main estimate.

The three irrigation groups are cut at 40 and 60 percent irrigated. The village source comparison enters the shares of villages relying on canals and on surface water together with irrigated share and its square, log mean holding size, the Scheduled Tribe population share and state fixed effects. The characteristic comparison enters each characteristic first on its own and then with irrigated share and its square, log mean holding size and state fixed effects, leaving out the holding term when the characteristic is holding size. Its facility measures are shares of a district's villages that have each facility.

The village source record is a single 2019–20 cross-section set against two decades of cropping. Cropping intensity built from these records sits about 0.3 crops a year above the official state figures and ranks the states only moderately alike (rank correlation 0.70).

The comparison of each district with itself uses net irrigated over net sown area from the district land-use statistics, 1998–99 to 2019–20, for 600 of the districts. Its 2017–19 average correlates with the village-record share at 0.87. With district and state-by-year fixed effects, the straight-line slope of the effective number of crops on the irrigated share is −0.225 (0.192), or −0.225 (0.251) with errors clustered by state. A quadratic in the same setting has a squared term of −1.78 (0.46), but nearly all of that curvature comes from districts with higher average irrigation having more negative slopes over time (−3.67, 0.95). Curvature along a district's own path is −1.06 (0.73), and with state clusters the squared term has p = 0.11. Lagging irrigation by a year weakens the curvature and next year's irrigation shows some of its own, which points to year-specific reporting moving both series. The comparison across districts in the same chart is the slope above half irrigated from the two-piece line.

Noise in the yearly irrigation figures. Year-on-year changes in a district's land-use irrigated share are negatively correlated (−0.30), as reporting noise that comes and goes would make them. Read that way, such noise makes up about a fifth of the variation the within-district slope uses, which on its own would barely move it. Slower errors are larger. Over 388 districts in 18 states with both records, the change in the land-use share from 2000–01 to 2002–03 to 2010–11 to 2012–13 and the change in the census village-directory share from 2001 to 2011 correlate at 0.13 within state, so if the two records err independently the signal is 12 percent of the land-use change (state bootstrap, 2 to 27 percent) and 15 percent of the census change. The third row of the chart divides the within-district slope and its interval by 0.12. In a long difference of the effective number of crops over the same years, the land-use change instrumented by the census change has a first-stage F of 4.0 and an estimate of +6.8 per unit (95 percent interval −2.2 to +15.7); the Anderson-Rubin interval, which stays valid with a weak instrument, has no upper end. The first stage stays weak, F between 1.8 and 7.1, in other windows and samples.

Irrigation has no source of variation that is both strong and plausibly unrelated to cropping. A district's starting mix of canal, tank and well irrigation, combined with each source's growth in other states, mostly tracks convergence in irrigation (first-stage F 3.5). Pre-monsoon groundwater depth from Central Ground Water Board wells starts in 2013, gives F between 2.9 and 6.5, and partly reflects the pumping it would predict.

Sources

Yearly irrigation comes from the district land-use statistics of the Directorate of Economics and Statistics. Cropping is district crop area statistics for 1998–99 to 2021–22.
