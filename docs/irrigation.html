Irrigation

How irrigation lines up with crop diversity

The effective number of crops is highest in districts that irrigate a moderate share of their sown area. When a district's own irrigation rises over the years, it barely moves.

01 · The curve

In a comparison within state (state fixed effects), the effective number of crops peaks where about 28 percent of sown area is irrigated.

Each dot is a district, shaded by its irrigation group, and the curve is a quadratic fit within state with a 95 percent band. Hover or tap a dot for the district.

The average district grows 21.4 crops in a year, yet its effective number of crops is only 4.9, because the largest crop alone usually holds close to half the land.

Set against every other district in the country, the effective number peaks at 51 percent irrigated. Within state, where districts share policy, rainfall and the way records are kept, the peak comes down to 28 percent, while the count of crops peaks later, at 46 percent.

Because canal commands and tubewell belts were built where soils, aquifers and terrain already favoured some kinds of farming, the curve describes how districts differ from one another. A district that added irrigation need not travel along it.

02 · Inside each district

When a district's own irrigated share rises over the years, its effective number of crops barely moves.

Net irrigated over net sown area, yearly land-use statistics, 1998–99 to 2019–20. Bands and whiskers are 95 percent intervals. In the second view, which sets 1998–2000 against 2017–19, filled dots have p < 0.05.

Across districts within state, the yearly land-use statistics show the same bend, with the effective number of crops highest at 27 percent of net sown area irrigated. Year by year the peak lies between 17 and 35 percent, and it has drifted up from about a quarter around 2000 to about a third by 2017–19.

Inside a district the line goes flat. Holding the district and its state's year fixed, 10 more percentage points of irrigation go with a change of between −0.06 and +0.02 in the effective number of crops, on a mean of 4.9. Districts that were already well irrigated show a more negative slope over time than drier ones, which is the bend across districts appearing again, although once uncertainty is allowed to run across whole states that curvature is not statistically significant (p = 0.11).

Over twenty years, rising irrigation went with the loss of minor crops. The irrigated share rose by about 12 points in the average district between 1998–2000 and 2017–19, and for every 10 points a district ended up with about a quarter of a crop fewer among those holding at least half a percent of its cropped land. Counting crops down to a quarter of a percent, the loss is a third of a crop. At 1 percent it is smaller and at 2 percent it vanishes, while the leading crop's share of land does not change.

None of this measures what irrigation itself does to cropping. Because the same state offices compile irrigation and crop area, an error in one can travel with the other. Neither a district's starting mix of canal, tank and well water nor the depth of its groundwater predicts later irrigation cleanly enough to stand in for an experiment. Comparing land downstream of dams by river gradient, as Duflo and Pande (2007) did, would need river slopes and a dated register of dams matched to these districts.

03 · Deciles of irrigation

The effective number of crops peaks at 5.9 in the fourth decile of districts by irrigation and falls to 3.7 in the most irrigated decile.

District means by decile of irrigated share, with whiskers of one standard error. Hover or tap a decile for its values.

With no curve fitted, the deciles run from 16.5 percent of sown area irrigated on average in the driest to 92.2 percent in the most irrigated. The effective number of crops climbs from 4.13 in the driest decile to 5.89 in the fourth, where irrigation averages 44 percent, then falls to 3.69 in the most irrigated, about 2.2 below the peak. A dip in the fifth decile and a recovery in the sixth are the kind of wobble one decile's mean can show by chance.

The count of crops peaks two deciles later, at 24.5 in the eighth, before falling to 18.1 in the most irrigated. Although the eighth decile grows the longest list of crops, it spreads its land least evenly, with an evenness of 0.212 against 0.282 in the fourth.

04 · Where

Districts in Punjab, Haryana and Uttar Pradesh irrigate more than four-fifths of their sown area on average.

Share of net sown area irrigated, 2019–20 village records. White lines are state borders and grey districts have no usable record. Hover or tap a district for its values, or a group in the key to pick out its districts.

The northwest and the Gangetic plain irrigate most of their sown area. Punjab averages 92 percent across its districts, Haryana 86 and Uttar Pradesh 84. The least irrigated districts are in Kerala, the Himalayan states, the northeast and Chhattisgarh. Kerala averages 23 percent and Himachal Pradesh 25.

Districts irrigating between 40 and 60 percent of their sown area average 5.58 on the effective number of crops, against 4.69 below 40 percent and 4.62 above 60, even though the count of crops barely differs across the three, from 20.7 to 21.6. On the irrigation figures of the 2011 census village directory the middle group again comes out highest, at 5.66.

05 · What the land is used for

Cereals take 79 percent of gross cropped area in the most irrigated decile of districts, against 58 percent in the seventh.

A crop group's share of gross cropped area, with whiskers of one standard error. The shaded band marks the three most irrigated deciles.

Cereals hold the largest share of cropped land in every decile. Their share falls from 0.718 in the driest decile to 0.583 in the seventh, then climbs to 0.777 in the ninth and 0.794 in the most irrigated as pulses and oilseeds give way. Pulse share drops from 0.140 in the eighth decile to 0.021 in the most irrigated, and oilseed share from 0.184 in the seventh to 0.041.

Over those three most irrigated deciles the effective number of crops falls from 4.99 to 3.69, so the districts where cereals gain are also the ones putting more of their land under fewer crops.

06 · How firm the bend is

The fitted curve bends downward in all fourteen versions of the estimate.

effective number of crops other measures of diversity headline

The turning point is the irrigated share at which the fitted curve peaks. Filled dots have p < 0.05 on the squared term, and every version except the first is within state.

A quadratic can show a peak where the true relationship only levels off. Changing the irrigation record, the set of districts or the weight each district carries never pushes the peak on the effective number of crops outside 24 to 35 percent irrigated within state, and the median version puts it at 27. What does move it is giving up the comparison within state. Set against every district in the country, the peak shifts out to 51 percent, which places the higher figure in differences between states.

Other measures of diversity bend as well, although their peaks spread more widely. Evenness, which ignores how many crops there are, peaks earliest, at 16 percent, and bends least firmly (p = 0.023), while the count of crops peaks latest, at 46 percent.

07 · Where the water comes from

Groundwater is the main irrigation source in half of a district's villages on average.

Mean share of a district's villages naming each source as their main one, 2019–20.

In the average district, groundwater is the main irrigation source in half of the villages, canals in 17 percent and surface water, meaning tanks, ponds and lifts from rivers, in 11 percent. The remaining fifth name no particular source.

Groundwater rises steadily with irrigation, from 23 percent of villages in the least irrigated decile of districts to 78 percent in the most irrigated, while tanks, ponds and unnamed sources are most common where irrigation is lowest. Canals matter most in the upper middle of the range, reaching 23 percent of villages in the seventh decile.

The sources deliver water on different terms. A canal runs on a schedule set by an irrigation authority, usually built around the crop the system was designed for, whereas a farmer with a tubewell decides when to run the pump, within the limits of the pump and the water table. Tanks and ponds hold only what that season's rain puts in them.

08 · Source at the same irrigated share

At the same irrigated share, districts where more villages rely on surface water have a lower effective number of crops and more land in cereals.

canal surface water p ≥ 0.05

Difference from a district where every village relies on groundwater or other sources, at the same irrigated share and within the same state. Whiskers are 95 percent intervals.

Among districts in the same state with the same irrigated share, similar farm sizes and similar Scheduled Tribe populations, the source of water still matters. Where surface water dominates, diversity is lower. A district in which every village relies on it has an effective number of crops 2.03 lower than one where none does (p = 0.029), a number of main crops 1.67 lower (p = 0.021) and a cereal share 0.30 higher (p = 0.0001), with pulse share 0.11 lower and oilseed share 0.15 lower.

Since surface water is the main source in only 11 percent of villages on average, a more realistic gap is 10 percentage points of villages, which goes with an effective number of crops about 0.2 lower and a cereal share 0.03 higher. Even so, the full-range cereal gap of 0.30 is wider than the whole spread of cereal share across the irrigation deciles, from 0.583 to 0.794.

Canal districts grow a shorter list of crops. Where every village relies on a canal, a district grows 2.61 fewer crops (p = 0.003), yet its effective number of crops is only 0.32 lower, which is not statistically significant (p = 0.42). Because the effective number weighs crops by land, the crops missing from canal districts most likely held little area. Their pulse share is 0.071 higher (p = 0.0007) and their oilseed share 0.085 lower (p = 0.006).

Because nothing records how reliable each source is, uncertain supply and canal scheduling cannot be told apart.

09 · Other district characteristics

Producer organisations and cropping intensity are the two district characteristics still linked to the effective number of crops within state once irrigation and holding size are held constant.

on its own adjusted, p < 0.05 adjusted, p ≥ 0.05

Adjusted means within state, with the irrigation curve and mean holding size held constant. Facilities are shares of villages, and values are per standard deviation of each characteristic.

Within state, and with irrigation and farm size held constant, two characteristics keep a clear association with the effective number of crops. A district where every village has a producer organisation scores 1.88 higher than one where none does (p = 0.029), or about 0.36 for one standard deviation in the share of villages that have one. Cropping intensity, gross cropped area over net sown area, carries +2.31 per unit (p < 0.0001), about 0.56 for one standard deviation.

Food storage warehouses, regular markets, canal dependence and the cultivator share of agricultural workers each line up with the effective number of crops on their own, but the association fades once districts are compared within state with irrigation held constant. Mean holding size drops from +0.35 on its own (p = 0.004) to +0.02 (p = 0.86). Because a district mean says little about how holdings are spread inside the district, it is a poor guide to farm size.

How this was estimated

The comparisons across districts use 606 districts with at least 10 years of crop records and a usable village record. Irrigation is the share of net sown area that village records list as irrigated in the 2019–20 Mission Antyodaya survey.

The four diversity measures are each computed within a year and averaged over 1998–99 to 2019–20. Their district means are 21.4 for the count of crops, 4.9 for the effective number, 3.5 for the number of main crops and 0.250 for evenness, and area shares are over gross cropped area.

The village-record share runs above published state irrigation figures in Maharashtra, Jharkhand and Assam. Leaving those three states out gives a squared term of −6.09 (p < 0.0001), with the peak at 21 percent irrigated. Against the 2011 census village directory the district measure agrees at a rank correlation of 0.81.

The model is D = b0 + b1 · irr + b2 · irr², with state fixed effects in every version but one. The turning point is −b1 / (2 · b2). The within-state curve in the first chart takes its intercept from the state fixed effects averaged over the districts, so its mean equals the sample mean of 4.95. R² is 0.067 across all districts together and 0.534 within state.

Standard errors allow for uneven scatter and group districts by the 2011 district whose village records they carry. Leaving out districts whose 2011 records also cover another district gives a squared term of −6.38, and weighting each district by one over the number of districts sharing its 2011 records gives −6.60, against −6.84 in the headline.

The three irrigation groups are cut at 40 and 60 percent irrigated. The source comparison enters the shares of villages relying on canals and on surface water together with the irrigation curve, log mean holding size, the Scheduled Tribe population share and state fixed effects. The characteristic comparison enters each characteristic first on its own and then with the irrigation curve, log mean holding size and state fixed effects, leaving out the holding term when the characteristic is holding size. Its facility measures are shares of a district's villages that have each facility.

The village source record is a single 2019–20 cross-section set against two decades of cropping, and cropping intensity agrees only moderately with published state figures.

The comparison of each district with itself uses net irrigated over net sown area from the district land-use statistics, 1998–99 to 2019–20, for 600 of the districts. Its 2017–19 average correlates with the village-record share at 0.87, and the cross-section of district means with state fixed effects puts the peak at 27.3 percent (standard error 4.7 points). With district and state-by-year fixed effects, the straight-line slope of the effective number of crops on the irrigated share is −0.225 (0.192), or −0.225 (0.251) with errors clustered by state. A quadratic in the same setting has a squared term of −1.78 (0.46), but nearly all of that curvature comes from districts with higher average irrigation having more negative slopes over time (−3.67, 0.95). Curvature along a district's own path is −1.06 (0.73), and with state clusters the squared term has p = 0.11. Lagging irrigation by a year weakens the bend and next year's irrigation shows one of its own, which points to year-specific reporting moving both series.

The twenty-year comparison sets 1998–2000 against 2017–19 in 406 districts with state fixed effects. Per unit of irrigated share, crops holding at least 0.5 percent of cropped area change by −2.52 (0.78), or −3.18 (0.83) with starting irrigation held constant. At thresholds of 0.25, 1 and 2 percent the change is −3.27 (0.78), −1.16 (0.69) and −0.24 (0.60). The effective number of crops changes by −0.44 (0.44) and the leading crop's share by −0.013 (0.034). Year on year within districts, the 0.5 percent count falls by 0.82 (0.29) per unit, and that disappears once each district has its own trend.

No source of variation in irrigation clears both hurdles of a strong first stage and a credible exclusion. Shift-share instruments built from each district's starting mix of canal, tank and well irrigation and each source's growth in other states mostly track convergence in irrigation, and with starting irrigation interacted with year their first stage F is 3.5. Pre-monsoon groundwater depth from Central Ground Water Board wells starts in 2013, gives F between 2.9 and 6.5, and partly reflects the pumping it would predict. The river-gradient design of Duflo and Pande (2007) needs slope and river-network layers and the national register of dams with completion dates.

Sources

Irrigated share, irrigation source and district characteristics come from village records in SHRUG 2.1 by the Development Data Lab, drawing on the Mission Antyodaya 2019–20 village survey and the 2011 census village directory. Yearly irrigation comes from the district land-use statistics of the Directorate of Economics and Statistics. Cropping is district crop area statistics for 1998–99 to 2019–20.
