Lens one

Irrigation and crop diversity

Across 606 districts the effective number of crops rises with the irrigated share of sown area and then falls. The peak sits between a quarter and a third of sown area in most specifications. Irrigation source is estimated separately from irrigation level.

The effective number of crops rises with the irrigated share of sown area and then falls. It averages 4.1 crops in the least irrigated tenth of districts, peaks at 5.9 in the fourth tenth, and falls to 3.7 in the most irrigated tenth. One mechanism runs in a single direction: assured water makes irrigated cereals viable and would, on its own, push other crops out of the rotation, which gives a steady decline.

The average district grows about 21 crops in a year, and one cereal usually takes most of the land, so the number of equally-sized crops that would give the same diversity is about 5. That second number is the effective number of crops: the Hill number of order one, the exponential of Shannon entropy. It moves when land shifts between crops even if the list of crops grown does not change at all.

Irrigation is measured from village records that report irrigated and unirrigated land separately, as irrigated land over the sum of the two. The measure is closed by construction and needs no external denominator. At state level it tracks published irrigation shares at a rank correlation of 0.85 with a mean absolute difference of 0.087, running high by 0.24 to 0.33 in Maharashtra, Jharkhand and Assam. An independent measure built from the 2011 census village directory agrees at a rank correlation of 0.81.

Everything on this page rests on 606 districts across 30 states. Each index is computed within a year and then averaged across the years the district is observed, a median of 23 years drawn from 1997–98 to 2019–20, with districts observed for fewer than 10 years left out.

Robustness

Curvature across specifications, samples and indices

The fitted line is allowed to bend once. The squared term on the irrigated share measures how hard it bends, and a negative value means it bends downward, so there is a peak somewhere inside the range. A quadratic fitted to a cross-section will often produce a turning point whether or not one exists, so the curvature was re-estimated fourteen ways.

Four variations change the sample: an irrigation measure built from the 2011 census village directory (573 districts), dropping districts that share a pre-2011 parent and therefore carry identical village records (542), dropping districts whose two irrigation sources disagree (530), and keeping only districts observed in all 23 years (506). Four keep all 606 districts and change the specification: state fixed effects in and out, inverse weighting by the number of sibling districts, and weighting by cropped area. Six change the diversity index.

State fixed effects mean each district is compared with districts in its own state, so Punjab is set against Punjab and not against Kerala, and anything a state shares (its policy, its rainfall regime, its recording practice) drops out. Without them the fitted turning point sits at 0.51 of sown area and the model accounts for 6 percent of the variation across districts. With them it sits at 0.28 and accounts for 53 percent.

Standard errors throughout are clustered on the pre-2011 parent district. 64 of the 606 districts were carved out of a shared parent and inherit identical village records, so they are not independent observations, and treating them as if they were would make the standard errors too small. Clustering widens them to allow for it. Two of the fourteen rows answer the same problem directly: dropping the shared-parent districts leaves the curvature at -6.38, and weighting each sibling group down leaves it at -6.61, against -6.88 in the headline.

The turning point is a ratio of the two estimated coefficients, so it moves with the index and with whether state fixed effects are included. Across the eight specifications on the effective number of crops it runs from 0.25 to 0.51 of sown area, with a median of 0.27. Across all fourteen rows it runs from 0.18 to 0.51. The sign of the curvature is negative in every one.

Read off the group means instead of the fitted line, the effective count peaks at 5.9 crops and falls to 3.7 in the most irrigated tenth of districts, a loss of about 2.2 effective crops.

The shape also appears on the plain count of crops, on both effective counts, and on evenness taken by itself. Evenness is measured with the count divided out, so the result there says irrigation is associated with how evenly land is divided between crops as well as with how many crops appear.

How to read the curve The curve describes how the cross-section is arranged. Canal commands and tubewell belts were developed where soils, aquifers, terrain and politics already favoured particular agriculture, and a district that installed irrigation tomorrow would not travel along it.

Composition

Where the water comes from

Village records report a dominant irrigation source alongside the irrigated share, and the sources deliver water on different terms. A canal command delivers on a schedule set by an authority, in a rotation built around the crop the system was designed for. Under a tubewell the timing is the farmer's, bounded by the pump and the water table. Surface sources, meaning tanks, ponds and lifts from rivers, hold only what the season's rainfall put in them.

Mission Antyodaya, a village survey run in 2019 covering most rural villages, records one dominant irrigation source per village. Averaged across a district's villages and then across districts, groundwater is dominant in 50.5 percent of villages, other sources in 21.1 percent, canal in 17.2 and surface water in 11.3. The 21.1 percent recorded as other is a residual class in the survey and does not name a technology. It is not entered separately below, so it sits with groundwater in the comparison. Coverage is thin in 26 districts where the survey reaches under a quarter of villages, concentrated in Tripura, Jammu and Kashmir and the island territories, and those districts sit outside the 606.

A village share is the fraction of a district's villages that report a given feature, counting each village once whatever its size. It is not a share of land and not a share of households. A district of many small villages and a district of few large ones therefore weigh the same, and a village drawing half its water from a canal and half from a tubewell counts wholly to whichever source is recorded as dominant.

Figure 5

Dominant irrigation source across villages

Mean share of a district's villages reporting each source as dominant, each village counted once whatever its size.

The source shares enter alongside irrigation and its square, with groundwater left out of the set. Every coefficient is therefore read against groundwater: a district whose villages report canal or surface dominance, set against a district on tubewells at the same irrigation level, in the same state and of similar holding size. Because the variable is a village share running from 0 to 1, a coefficient describes the difference between a district where no village reports that source as dominant and one where every village does, which covers the full range of the variable.

An area share is the crop group's area over the district's gross cropped area, averaged over the years the district is observed. Cereals take most of it nearly everywhere, running from 0.574 of sown area in the seventh tenth of districts by irrigation to 0.793 in the most irrigated tenth.

Cereal share across the ten irrigation groups runs from 0.718 down to 0.574 and back up to 0.793, a swing of about 0.22, so a coefficient of 0.30 spans roughly twice that whole observed range.

Canals shorten the crop list by 2.6 crops while leaving the effective count where it was, so the land does not concentrate onto fewer crops. Cereal concentration sits with surface water instead, at 0.30 of area. Reading that as a matter of how controllable the water is remains an interpretation: nothing here measures reliability of supply. Both results are cross-sectional, and the design does not separate a risk mechanism from a scheduling one.

Context

District characteristics and the effective number of crops

Irrigation is one of several district characteristics that could be lined up against diversity. Placing them on a common footing, each entered on its own and then again with the irrigated share, its square, log mean holding size and state fixed effects, shows which characteristics keep an association once irrigation, holding size and state are held constant.
