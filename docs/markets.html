Lens two

Markets, inputs, and the one institution that behaves differently

Rural market infrastructure is usually treated as a single quantity called access. It is not one thing, and the pieces pull in opposite directions.

A farmer's cropping decision is bounded by what can be sold and what can be bought. Both sides of that constraint are physical infrastructure sitting in specific villages: a regulated market yard, a weekly gathering in a field, a shop selling urea, a cold store. The village records in this data locate all of them, which makes it possible to ask what kind of market a district's agriculture is arranged around rather than simply how much market it has.

The distinction matters because the institutions are built for different transactions. A regulated mandi exists to move graded, storable commodity in bulk, which in most of India means cereals under assured procurement. A weekly haat clears small quantities of perishables between people who came on foot. A fertiliser shop is the retail end of a purchased-input package. A cold store makes a perishable sellable at all. Treating these as interchangeable measures of access loses the entire question.

Structure

Nine facilities and an outlier

Before asking what any of this is associated with, it is worth looking at how the facilities co-locate. If they all appear together, there is really only one variable here and it is called development. That is very nearly true.

Where the weekly haat is common

Periodic markets concentrate in the eastern and central belt and in the northeast, which is close to the inverse of where the input-supply network is dense.

Nine of the ten facilities load onto one factor, correlating with each other in a band that runs from moderate to very strong. The weekly haat does not. It correlates negatively with regulated mandis, with regular markets and with custom hiring, at around zero with night-time lights, and negatively with the density of non-farm establishments.

That is a substantive result rather than a data quirk. The haat is not a lesser version of a mandi on the way to becoming one. It sits in a different kind of place, and it is the only piece of rural market infrastructure in this data that is not simply a marker of commercial development. Everything that follows depends on that separation, which is also why the models below carry night-time lights, non-farm establishment density and a connectivity index: without them, any facility would mostly be standing in for prosperity.

Two patterns come out of those two figures and both survive entering all ten facilities simultaneously.

Weekly haats go with more crops

Districts where a larger share of villages hold a weekly haat grow more distinct crops. The association is stable across every variation tried, including one that controls for the other nine facilities at once, so it is not the haat standing in for general market access.

The magnitude deserves care. A one standard deviation difference in haat coverage corresponds to roughly one additional crop against a district mean of about twenty. This is a real and robust association, and it is small. It is also, importantly, an association with the count and not with the balance: the effective number of crops barely moves, and evenness if anything declines slightly.

What that combination means

More crops grown, no change in effective diversity, evenness flat to falling. Haat districts are adding crops at the margin, on small plots, around a staple that stays dominant. The periodic market is supporting a garden economy alongside the main field crop rather than restructuring the field crop itself. A composite index would have reported this as "haats make districts diverse", which is a stronger and less accurate claim.

Input and commercial infrastructure goes with fewer pulses

Fertiliser shops, cold storage and regular markets are each associated with a smaller share of land under pulses, consistently and at conventional significance. The fertiliser-shop result survives a correction for testing across all ten crop categories. Pulses are the crop group displaced where the purchased-input economy is denser, which is the pattern that the agronomic literature on cereal intensification would predict.

Producer organisations run the other way

Farmer producer organisations are the only facility positively associated with the effective number of crops, and they go with a smaller cereal share and larger shares under vegetables and fruit. Notably they lift the effective count without lifting the count of crops grown, which is the mirror image of the haat result: haats add crops at the margin, producer organisations rebalance the land already in use. Only an index family that keeps count and balance apart can distinguish the two.

Two things that did not hold

Hypotheses that failed

Both of the following were set out before estimation and neither survived. They are here because a lens that only reports what worked is not a lens.

Mandis do not push districts toward cereals

The expectation was straightforward: where assured procurement is physically close, the crops it procures should take more land. The association runs the other way. Within a state and at a given level of irrigation, denser mandi coverage goes with a somewhat smaller cereal share. Whatever regulated markets are doing to cropping patterns, it is not the simple thing.

Market infrastructure does not sharpen the irrigation turn

Diversity rises with irrigation and then falls. The standard account of the falling arm is that heavy irrigation plus assured procurement plus dense input supply makes the cereal package the only rational choice, which implies the fall should be steeper where that infrastructure is dense. Interacting each infrastructure measure with irrigation and its square gives the opposite sign in every case: the curve is flatter, not steeper, where markets and input supply are dense, and none of the interactions is convincingly distinguishable from zero.

If the procurement account were right, the filled markers would sit to the left of the hollow ones. They sit to the right in all five cases.

Reading these associations

Mandis, cold stores and fertiliser dealerships are built where there is already surplus to trade, and the causality plausibly runs in both directions with cropping pattern. Controlling for night-time lights, non-farm density and connectivity narrows that problem without resolving it. These are descriptions of how the cross-section is arranged.
