Lens two

Market and input facilities against district cropping patterns

Ten kinds of rural facility, from regulated mandis to weekly haats to fertiliser shops, measured as the share of a district's villages reporting each one and set against what the district grows. Across 606 districts in 30 states.

What a district grows is bounded by the output markets and the input suppliers within reach of its villages. Both sides of that constraint are physical infrastructure sitting in specific villages: a regulated mandi, a weekly haat, a fertiliser shop, a cold store. The village records locate all of them, so each facility is measured separately instead of summed into one access score.

The four institutions carry different transactions. A regulated mandi moves graded, storable commodity in bulk, which in most of India means cereals under assured procurement. Perishables in small lots clear at the weekly haat, which sits in the village itself and runs one day in seven. Fertiliser shops, seed centres, soil testing and custom hiring of machinery are the retail end of a purchased-input package, and cold storage is what makes a perishable sellable outside its harvest window.

Four of the ten need a word. Custom hiring is the village-level renting out of tractors and machinery to farmers who own none. Farm-gate processing is milling, ginning or grading done inside the village rather than after the produce has travelled. Seed centres and soil testing are extension services, the first supplying certified seed and the second telling a farmer what his field is short of. A farmer producer organisation is a registered collective that aggregates its members' produce and buys their inputs jointly, so it sits on both sides of the market at once.

Structure

How the ten facilities correlate with each other

Nine of the ten facilities correlate with each other between 0.24 and 0.83, so they largely stand in for one underlying quantity: how commercially developed a district's countryside is. The weekly haat is the exception, correlating between −0.21 and +0.06 with the other nine.

Where the weekly haat is common

Weekly haats are commonest in the eastern and central belt and in the northeast. Their correlation with the input-supply facilities is close to zero, between −0.10 and −0.02, and negative against regulated mandis at −0.15 and regular markets at −0.21.

Within the nine, the six input-supply and post-harvest measures sit closest together, between 0.63 and 0.83. The weekly haat sits apart from all of them. Its correlation is −0.15 with regulated mandis, −0.21 with regular markets and −0.09 with custom hiring. Against the development controls it runs +0.04 with night-time lights and −0.16 with the density of non-farm establishments, the only facility of the ten with a negative reading on either.

The weekly haat sits in a different kind of district. It is the only one of the ten facilities that does not rise with the density of non-farm business.

Because nine of the ten rise together with commercial development, every model carries three measures of how developed a district's countryside is: night-time light output from satellite imagery, which tracks local economic activity; non-farm establishments per thousand people from the Economic Census; and a connectivity index built from the share of villages with a paved road, public transport, a bank and a railway station. Without them a facility would largely be standing in for how prosperous its district is.

Association

What each facility goes with

Two outcomes measure diversity. The first is the plain count of crops a district grows in a year, which averages 21.2. The second is the effective number of crops, the exponential of the Shannon entropy: how many equally sized crops it would take to produce the diversity actually observed. That averages 4.9. A district growing twenty crops where one cereal takes most of the land has an effective count of four or five, because the nineteen crops on slivers barely register. When the count moves and the effective number does not, crops are appearing on small plots without changing the main field crop.

Each facility is entered on its own, against four outcomes: the count of crops a district grows, the effective number of crops, and the share of gross cropped area under cereals and under pulses. Crop shares are each category's share of a district's gross cropped area, pooled across 1997–98 to 2019–20.

Every model holds the share of sown area irrigated and its square, log mean holding size, scheduled tribe population share, night-time lights, non-farm establishment density and a connectivity index, and carries a separate intercept for each state. Each state getting its own intercept means a district is only ever compared with other districts of the same state. Nothing in these numbers comes from Punjab being unlike Kerala; it comes from districts within Punjab, and within Kerala, differing from one another.

Many present-day districts were carved out of a single pre-2011 parent and inherit identical village records, so they are not independent observations. Standard errors are clustered on that parent district, which widens them. 64 of the 606 districts sit on a shared parent, and dropping them entirely leaves the haat coefficient at +8.0.

Because the facilities correlate up to 0.83 with one another, entering them together splits shared variance and makes individual coefficients unstable enough to flip sign. Entering the four input-supply facilities as a block pushes variance inflation to between 3.4 and 4.1. Entering each on its own is the honest read: it asks what a district with more of that facility looks like, without pretending to hold the others constant.

Coefficients are per unit of the village share, which is the gap between no village having the facility and every village having one. That contrast never occurs in the data, the highest district mean being 23 percent of villages, so the readings that follow are also given per one standard deviation of the share, which for the weekly haat is 11.7 percentage points of villages.

Weekly haats go with more crops

Districts where a larger share of villages hold a weekly haat grow more distinct crops. The coefficient runs from +6.4 to +8.0 across six adjusted specifications: with state fixed effects alone, adding irrigation, with the full control set, on a sample that drops districts sharing a pre-2011 parent, on a sample that drops the 30 districts reporting no haat, and with all nine other facilities entered alongside. Measuring haats per hundred thousand people instead of as a village share gives the same sign at p = 0.008.

Before any controls the haat coefficient on crop count is +17.0. State fixed effects alone cut it to +7.2 and it holds there through the full control set at +7.3. Most of the raw association is geography rather than the haat, and what survives is what is left within a state.

A one standard deviation difference in haat coverage, 11.7 percentage points of a district's villages, goes with 0.9 more crops grown, against a mean of 21.2 crops.

The count of crops moves and the balance does not. The effective number of crops rises by 0.49 (p = 0.56) against a mean of 4.9. Evenness, the effective count divided by the plain count, says how equally area is spread across the crops that are grown with the number of crops divided out, and it averages 0.254; with the three output-market facilities entered together it falls by 0.071 (p = 0.05).

What that combination means

Haat districts grow more crops without spreading their area any more evenly across them. The extra crops sit on small plots around a staple that keeps most of the area, closer to kitchen gardening than to a change in the main field crop. Keeping the count and the balance as separate outcomes is what makes that distinction visible.

Input and commercial infrastructure goes with fewer pulses

Fertiliser shops go with a pulse share 17.4 percentage points lower (p < 0.001), cold storage 17.2 points lower (p = 0.008) and regular markets 9.4 points lower (p = 0.004), each with the facility entered on its own. With the four input-supply facilities entered together, the fertiliser-shop coefficient on pulse share is −19.0 percentage points.

Each facility is tested against ten crop categories, so at five percent one of them would be expected to clear the bar by chance. A Benjamini-Hochberg correction across the ten adjusts for that, and the fertiliser shop result on pulses survives it. Pulses are the crop group that gives way where fertiliser shops, seed centres and custom hiring of machinery are dense.

Producer organisations go with a higher effective number of crops

Farmer producer organisations are the only facility of the ten whose association with the effective number of crops is distinguishable from zero. The effective number of crops is 1.95 higher (p = 0.032) while the count of crops grown moves by 2.39 and is not distinguishable from zero (p = 0.19). Area is spread more evenly across the crops a district already grows. With cold storage and farm-gate processing entered alongside, producer organisations go with a cereal share 22.2 percentage points lower, a vegetable share 5.7 points higher and a fruit share 3.2 points higher.

Two things that did not hold

Mandi density and the shape of the irrigation curve

Two expectations were set out before estimation and the cross-section does not support either.

Mandis do not push districts toward cereals

Denser mandi coverage goes with a smaller cereal share. Within a state and at a given level of irrigation, a district whose villages all held a mandi would sit 23.5 percentage points lower on cereal area (p = 0.054) than one with none. That contrast never occurs: the average district has a mandi in 3.0 percent of its villages, and 40 districts have none at all.

The irrigation curve is flatter where market and input infrastructure is dense

The effective number of crops is fitted as a curve in the share of sown area irrigated: it rises, peaks near 28 percent irrigated, and falls after. The coefficient on the squared term is what bends the line, so a more negative value is a sharper peak. Interacting that squared term with each infrastructure measure asks whether the bend is sharper where infrastructure is dense.

The usual explanation for the falling arm is Punjab: where irrigation is near total and procurement is assured, wheat and rice are the settled choice. If that were the mechanism, the fall would be steeper where mandis and fertiliser shops are dense. Every interaction is positive, so the curve is flatter where markets and input supply are dense. The mandi interaction is +2.62 (p = 0.033), the input-supply index +2.23 (p = 0.056) and the post-harvest index +2.10 (p = 0.087); the fertiliser shop and output-market interactions do not clear ten percent.

Reading these associations

Mandis, cold stores and fertiliser shops are built where there is already surplus to trade, so causality runs in both directions with the cropping pattern. Controlling for night-time lights, non-farm density and connectivity narrows that problem without resolving it. Facilities are recorded in 2019–20 and cropping is averaged across 1997–98 to 2019–20, so these are associations between a district's current infrastructure and its long-run cropping pattern.
