Markets and inputs

How rural markets and input shops line up with crop diversity

Districts where more people live near a weekly haat grow more crops without spreading their land more evenly, and districts where more people have a farmer collective in the village, a producer organisation or a cooperative credit society, do both. Most other facilities follow state lines.

01 · What exists

In the average district, one rural resident in 28 lives in a village with a mandi, and one in five in a village with a weekly haat.

Each dot is one in a hundred of a district's rural people. Low and high are the 10th and 90th percentiles of districts.

Because larger villages tend to be better served, a facility reaches a bigger share of a district's rural people than of its villages. A fertiliser shop is within the village for 25.4 percent of rural people in the average district.

The mandi, the regulated wholesale market where crops are auctioned under state rules, is the rarest facility, and 40 districts have none at all. Weekly haats, open-air markets that meet on a fixed day, reach about five times as many people. Farmer collectives are the most widespread of all, reaching 28 percent of rural people and missing from only five districts. That reach makes sense once the survey question is read: it asks whether the village has any farmers' collective and counts either a farmer producer organisation or a primary agricultural credit society, the village cooperative that gives crop loans. Across districts the measure follows the 2011 census record of agricultural credit societies closely (a correlation of 0.63), so it says more about cooperatives in general than about producer organisations.

Supply varies widely between districts. A fertiliser shop reaches 6 percent of rural people in a district at the 10th percentile and 55 percent at the 90th.

02 · How they group

Weekly haats are common in different districts from the other nine facilities.

Lines join facilities whose shares correlate across districts at 0.4 or more, and thicker lines mean higher correlation. The weekly haat is drawn closer to the others than its correlations would place it, so that it fits. Hover or tap a facility to read its correlations.

Nine of the facilities tend to turn up in the same districts, with correlations between them of 0.09 to 0.80. Seed centres, soil testing, custom hiring of machinery, fertiliser shops, farm-gate processing and farmer collectives form the tightest cluster, every pair at 0.57 or more, and custom hiring and farmer collectives are the closest pair.

Mandis, regular markets and weekly haats are all places where farmers sell, yet the weekly haat runs against the other two. Its correlation is −0.34 with regular markets and −0.22 with mandis, and with the input-supply facilities it sits between −0.15 and −0.05. Where weekly haats reach many people, the other kinds of market tend to be scarce.

Part of that gap comes from the survey itself, which takes one answer per village for its market, so a village with a mandi or a regular market cannot also count as a haat village. The 2011 census allowed both, and there almost half the villages with a weekly haat also had a mandi or regular market. Measured that way, haat and other-market reach go together across districts, at a correlation of +0.42.

03 · Where weekly haats are

Weekly haats reach the most people across eastern and central India and the northeast.

Share of rural people living in a village with each facility. The top and bottom 2 percent of districts take the end colours of the scale, and hatched districts have no facility record.

In Chhattisgarh 44 percent of rural people live in a village with a weekly haat, and in Jharkhand 35 percent. Maharashtra, Meghalaya, Bihar, West Bengal and Assam follow at 26 to 31 percent. Fertiliser shops and farmer collectives reach the most people in Kerala and Gujarat.

04 · Development and state

Weekly haats are the one facility that is scarcer in districts with more non-farm businesses.

with non-farm businesses with night lights

Correlation across districts between each facility's share and either night-light brightness seen from satellite, on a log scale, or non-farm businesses per 1,000 people.

Night lights track economic activity, and the count of non-farm businesses covers shops and other enterprises outside farming. Custom hiring of machinery, fertiliser shops, regular markets and farmer collectives are all more common where there are more such businesses, at +0.31 to +0.33. The weekly haat runs the other way, at −0.18, and has almost no link with night lights.

Because richer districts have more of nearly everything, the links with cropping below hold night lights, non-farm businesses, road, bank and rail access, irrigation, farm size and the share of people from Scheduled Tribes constant. Each is also a comparison within state (state fixed effects), so that a crop pattern common to a whole state is not read as a link with any facility.

05 · Counting crops two ways

A district can grow 20 crops and still have an effective number of crops close to five.

Illustration with invented shares. Land shares are shares of gross cropped area, which counts a field twice if it carries two crops in a year.

The average district grows 21.4 crops, yet its effective number of crops, which weighs each crop by its land, is only 4.9.

The example district grows 20 crops with 62 percent of its land under one cereal, which gives it the same effective number of 4.9. A twenty-first crop on one percent of the land raises the count by one but the effective number only to 5.1, whereas moving ten percent of the land from the cereal to the next four largest crops leaves the count at 20 and lifts the effective number to 6.0.

06 · District by district

Chhattisgarh and Assam grow close to 30 crops per district, with an effective number of crops near three.

Crop measures are district averages over 1998–99 to 2019–20, facilities are shares of rural people with one in the village, and hatched districts have no usable record. Hover or tap a district for its value.

Among the larger states, Chhattisgarh grows the most crops, 31.9 per district on average, ahead of Karnataka at 30.1 and Assam at 29.2. Punjab's districts grow 9.1.

On the effective number of crops the brightest districts move south. Karnataka leads at 8.1, followed by Tamil Nadu at 7.0 and Andhra Pradesh at 6.9, while Odisha is lowest at 2.4. Chhattisgarh drops to 3.1 and Assam to 3.2, because rice is the largest crop in every one of their districts and covers about three quarters of the land, leaving the many other crops to share what is left.

Fertiliser shops and farmer collectives reach far more people in Kerala, 86 and 87 percent, and in Gujarat, 60 and 66 percent, than anywhere else.

07 · States

About half the spread in the effective number of crops across districts lies between states.

Each dot is a district and each white tick a state average. The dashed line is the average across districts. The bar is the share of the spread across districts that lies between states.

Karnataka's districts average 8.1 on the effective number of crops and Odisha's 2.4, even though inside Karnataka alone they run from 3.8 to 14.3. If every district took its state's average, it would keep 49 percent of the spread in the effective number of crops. For the count of crops it would keep 82 percent, which means a district's state goes most of the way to predicting how many crops it grows.

Facilities divide along state lines too, with between 60 and 77 percent of the spread in fertiliser shops, farmer collectives, custom hiring of machinery, farm-gate processing, regular markets and seed centres lying between states. Set side by side across the whole country, districts would mostly be compared as members of their states, which is why each link between facilities and cropping is read within state.

08 · Six groups of district

The best-supplied districts have a higher effective number of crops, a gap that mostly comes from the states they are in.

Shading: a district's supply of the nine facilities other than the weekly haat, as its average percentile rank across them, cut into thirds. Hatching: more than 17.9 percent of rural people, the median, live in a village with a weekly haat. On each card the white dot is the group's average and the faint ticks show the other five groups. Select a card to show its group alone.

Districts differ in two ways at once: how well supplied they are with the nine facilities that rise and fall together, and whether weekly haats reach more people than in the median district. Since weekly haats bear almost no relation to the other facilities (a correlation of −0.06), haat-rich districts turn up among the worst and the best supplied alike. Nor do districts fall into natural clusters, so the six groups are cuts through a continuous spread.

Supply climbs steeply from the bottom third to the top. A fertiliser shop reaches 11 to 13 percent of rural people in a bottom-third district and 40 to 45 percent in the top third, and farmer collectives go from 11 to 14 percent up to 46 percent. Over the same thirds the effective number of crops rises from about 4.3 to between 5.4 and 6.4, while cereals' share of the land falls from 75 to 80 percent down to 52 to 57 percent.

Most of that gap goes with the states the districts sit in. Within state, the thirds differ in the effective number of crops by amounts that are not statistically significant (p = 0.053 for the three together), and state accounts for 49 percent of the spread against 9 percent for the six groups. Cereal share holds up better within state, where middle- and top-third districts put about 4 points less of their land under cereals than bottom-third ones (p = 0.001). The top third is no lower than the middle, though, and only one of the four diversity and crop measures compared shows such a gap, so it needs confirming before it carries weight.

The weekly haat split matters more for the count of crops. Within each third the haat-rich half grows 3.0 to 4.8 more crops, and in the bottom and middle thirds it spreads its land less evenly, with an evenness of 0.20 to 0.23 against 0.26 to 0.29. Within state the haat-rich half still grows 1.1 more crops (p = 0.006), while its effective number of crops is 0.16 higher, which is not statistically significant (p = 0.36).

09 · The result

Districts where more people live near a weekly haat grow more crops without spreading their land more evenly among them.

Count of crops

Effective number of crops

Change from no rural resident to all of them having the facility in the village, within state and with development, irrigation, farm size and Scheduled Tribe share held constant. Filled dots have p < 0.05; lines are 95 percent intervals.

Each coefficient is the change in crop diversity as a facility's reach goes from no one to everyone, a far wider gap than separates most districts. One standard deviation, the typical distance of a district from the average, is a more realistic step, and for weekly haats it is 15 percent of rural people.

On the count of crops the weekly haat has the largest coefficient, +6.28, so a district one standard deviation above average grows about 0.9 more crops, against an average of 21.4. Its coefficient on the effective number of crops is +0.55, which is not statistically significant (p = 0.44). That is what you would expect if the extra crops sit on small plots while one staple still covers most of the land. Where everyone has a weekly haat, evenness is 0.051 lower once all three output markets are taken together, although that too falls short of statistical significance (p = 0.094).

Farmer collectives go with more of both. Their coefficient on the effective number of crops is +1.89 (p = 0.017), about 0.4 for one standard deviation, and on the count of crops +3.38 (p = 0.024), although once districts in the same state are allowed to move together the first falls short of significance (p = 0.17). Because the survey counts a primary agricultural credit society as a collective, this is mainly a link with village cooperatives, and the 2011 census count of agricultural credit societies gives a similar coefficient, +2.67 on the effective number. It says little about farmer producer organisations as such. Seed centres, food storage warehouses and farm-gate processing also go with a longer list of crops, at +4.4 to +5.3, whereas districts where more people live near a regular market grow fewer (−3.48, p = 0.040).

10 · How firm the weekly haat result is

The weekly haat link to the count of crops stays between +5.2 and +6.9 across six adjusted versions of the comparison.

Weekly haat coefficient on the count of crops: the change from no rural resident to all of them living in a village with one. Every p is 0.0001 or below.

Before any adjustment the weekly haat coefficient is +14.42. Within state it falls to +6.02, so most of the raw link reflects where weekly haats are, in eastern and central India. After that it hardly moves. Whether irrigation and development are held constant, districts carved from a split 2011 district or those with no haat at all are set aside, or the nine other facilities enter the same comparison, it stays between +5.2 and +6.9, which means the link does not hang on any one control or any one group of districts. Counted as weekly haats per 100,000 people, each extra haat goes with 0.055 more crops (p = 0.007).

11 · What the extra crops are

Most of the extra crops in haat-rich districts each cover less than a thousandth of the cropped area.

Within state, with all measures held constant. Filled dots stay below p = 0.05 when districts in the same state are allowed to move together. Crop by crop: the change in the share of years from 1998–99 to 2019–20 in which a district grows the crop, for the 22 crops that pass a Benjamini-Hochberg correction.

If only crops holding at least 0.1 percent of a district's cropped area are counted, the haat coefficient falls from +6.28 to +2.46, and once districts in a state are allowed to move together it is no longer statistically significant (p = 0.16). At a 0.5 percent floor it is −0.06 and at 1 percent −0.22. The extra crops are thin ones, grown on a few plots and sold in small lots, which fits a market where a farmer can bring a basket on the weekly day.

Crop by crop, haat-rich districts grow sunflower, safflower and linseed in more of their years, along with turmeric, coriander, onion, several pulses and a little wheat. The twenty crops that gain cover a median 0.4 percent of cropped area, and only sugarcane and sunflower also gain land. Guar and moth bean, both crops of the dry west, are grown less often. Turmeric, banana and coriander are among the six crops the source stops reporting after 2019–20, yet with all six left out the count coefficient is still +5.17.

The 2011 census tells a fuller story. Because Mission Antyodaya records one market per village, its haat villages are those without a mandi or regular market. In the census, which counts a haat wherever there is one, haat reach goes with more crops above the 0.5 percent floor (+2.11) and with a higher effective number of crops (+1.86, about 0.37 for one standard deviation), and both hold when districts in a state move together (p = 0.03). The thin-crop pattern belongs to villages whose only market is the weekly haat.

12 · Which crops

Districts with more fertiliser shops put less of their land under pulses.

Change in pulse share, percentage points, if every rural resident had the facility in the village

Share of cropped area over 1998–99 to 2019–20, one comparison per facility. One standard deviation of fertiliser-shop reach is 19.2 percent of rural people, and filled dots have p < 0.05.

In the average district pulses cover 10.2 percent of cropped area and cereals 66 percent. Fertiliser shops go with a pulse share 12.2 percentage points lower if every rural resident had one in the village, or about 2.3 points for one standard deviation more, close to a quarter of the average district's pulse land. Districts where more people live near a regular market also put less land under pulses, by 8.2 points.

With this many facilities and ten crop categories, some links will look significant by chance. The pulse links hold up after a Benjamini-Hochberg correction for that, whose q value reads like p: fertiliser shops come out at −14.4 points when the four input-supply facilities are compared together (q = 0.003), and regular markets at q = 0.005. Weekly haats pass too, with 10.1 points more land under sugar crops (q = 0.003).

Fertiliser shops also go with less land under cereals, 22.3 points less in the input-supply comparison (q = 0.025), and with more under fibre crops, mostly cotton, at +13.0 points (q = 0.025).

The fertiliser-shop links most likely run from crops to shops. A dealer opens where farmers already buy fertiliser in quantity, and cotton is among the most input-hungry crops grown in India, so districts given over to cotton draw shops whether or not the shops changed anything. Read these links as a map of where input demand sits.

No single state carries the sugar link. Leaving out one state at a time keeps it between +8.1 and +11.2 points, and dropping Maharashtra barely moves it (+10.0). It does lean on the main cane states, though: without Uttar Pradesh, Uttarakhand and Gujarat together it falls to +3.5 points (p = 0.012).

13 · Mandis and cereals

Mandis show no clear link to how much of a district's land goes to cereals.

Change in cereal share of cropped area, in percentage points, from no rural resident to all of them having the facility in the village, within state with all measures held constant. Filled dots have p < 0.05; lines are 95 percent intervals.

Mandis, where the state buys grain at support prices, are often expected to pull districts toward cereals. Yet where everyone lives near one, the cereal share is 15.3 points lower, a difference that is not statistically significant (p = 0.17). One standard deviation of mandi reach, 5.5 percent of rural people, would move cereal share by less than a point against an average of 66 percent. Fertiliser shops have the one clear cereal link, at −21.6 points (p = 0.003).

14 · Reading these results

These are associations between facilities recorded in 2019–20 and long-run cropping.

Cropping: district crop area, 1998–99 to 2019–20. Facilities: Mission Antyodaya 2019–20.

Facilities may well be built where there is already a surplus to trade, so a district's crops can shape its markets as much as the other way round. The timing makes the direction harder to read, because facilities are recorded in 2019–20 while the cropping measures average every year from 1998–99 to 2019–20. Whether a new weekly haat or farmer collective would change what farmers grow remains open.

Census records allow one check over time. Between 1991 and 2011 the share of rural people living in a village with a weekly market rose from 13 to 25 percent in the average district with usable records in both years. Districts where it rose more did not gain more crops between 1998–2003 and 2009–14, once the change in irrigation is held constant and districts are compared within state. One standard deviation more growth in haat reach, 15 points, goes with 0.15 more crops (95 percent interval −0.19 to +0.48) and 0.09 on the effective number (−0.04 to +0.22). Agricultural credit societies, the one market institution the 2001 census also records, barely changed in reach over the decade and show no link that survives once districts in a state move together. The intervals are wide enough that the change over time neither confirms the link across districts nor rules it out.

How this was estimated

The sample is 606 districts in 30 states with both a long-run crop record and a 2019–20 facility record. Each facility is the share of a district's rural population, counted in the 2011 census, that lives in a village reporting the facility in the Mission Antyodaya 2019–20 survey. Across districts these population shares correlate with plain village shares at 0.92 to 0.97, and weighting by the survey's own 2019–20 population gives shares that correlate with village shares just as closely. Each group average puts its facilities' shares on a common scale (distance from the mean in standard deviations) and averages them. Output markets combine mandis, regular markets and weekly haats, and input supply combines fertiliser shops, seed centres, soil testing and custom hiring of machinery. The post-harvest average covers food storage warehouses and farm-gate processing, and the connectivity average covers paved roads, public transport, banks and railways, all on the same population basis.

A regular market is a permanent local market, recorded apart from mandis and weekly haats.

The outcomes are the count of crops (mean 21.4), the effective number of crops (mean 4.9) and evenness (mean 0.250), all defined on the Measurement page, and each crop category's share of gross cropped area with all years from 1998–99 to 2019–20 added together (cereals 0.66, pulses 0.10). The diversity measures are computed within each year and then averaged across years. Each adjusted model controls for the share of sown area irrigated and its square, log mean holding size, the Scheduled Tribe population share, log night lights, non-farm businesses per 1,000 people, and the connectivity average, with state fixed effects.

The uncertainty ranges allow some districts to be more scattered than others, and districts split from the same 2011 district are treated as one group because their figures are likely to rise and fall together. Leaving out every district that shares its 2011 district with another puts the weekly haat result at +6.91.

The main results put one facility in each comparison, because the facilities overlap so much that putting them together blurs each one's estimate. Facilities are also compared together in groups: the output markets, the input-supply facilities, and food storage warehouses, farm-gate processing and farmer collectives. For each facility in those groups, q values apply a Benjamini-Hochberg correction across the ten crop categories.

Measured by the share of villages in place of the share of people, the weekly haat coefficient is +7.36 on the count of crops and +0.46 on the effective number, and farmer collectives are +1.92 on the effective number. On village shares mandis go with a cereal share 23.5 points lower (p = 0.054) and food storage warehouses with a pulse share 17.1 points lower (p = 0.009). Neither reaches p < 0.05 on population shares, and the fertiliser-shop link to cereals is clearer on population shares.

The farmer collective item is question 12 of the Mission Antyodaya 2019–20 village schedule, "Does the village has any Farmers Collective", answered as Farmers Produce Organization (FPOs), Primary Agriculture Cooperative Society (PACS), both, or none. A village counts as having one for any of the first three answers.

Letting the irrigation curve bend differently with each facility's reach gives a gentler fall at high irrigation where mandis reach more people: a curvature of −8.21 at one standard deviation below the mean and −4.06 at one above (p = 0.060 on population shares, 0.030 on village shares). Five measures were tried, so a p of that size is weak evidence. No measure steepens the fall: fertiliser shops make no difference (p = 0.95) and the output-market, input-supply and post-harvest averages give p between 0.24 and 0.69.

The check over time uses the census village directories. The 1991 directory records a market and its day of the week, the 2001 directory records no market at all, and the 2011 directory records weekly haats and mandis or regular markets as separate items, so haats are compared between 1991 (a market held on a named weekday) and 2011 (a weekly haat). Daily markets in 1991 are a poor match for mandis and regular markets in 2011, whose reach is eight times larger, and enter only as a control. Agricultural credit societies are compared between 2001 and 2011. Each round is weighted by the same 2011 village populations, only villages reporting the item in both rounds enter, and a district needs three quarters of its rural people in such villages. The 1991 market record is missing or thin for Gujarat, Haryana, Maharashtra, Odisha, Assam and Jammu and Kashmir, which leaves 338 districts in 17 states. Cropping is averaged over 1998–99 to 2002–03 and 2009–10 to 2013–14, and irrigation change is the census share irrigated from 2001 to 2011. Uncertainty treats districts split from one 2011 district as a group, and a wild bootstrap by state gives the same reading.

The six groups of district rest on two measures. Facility supply is a district's average percentile rank on the nine facilities other than the weekly haat, cut into thirds at 0.40 and 0.59. The weekly haat split is at the median share, 0.179, and the two measures correlate at −0.06. A k-means search on the facility shares, for three to six groups under three scalings and once more without the weekly haat, gives silhouette scores between 0.15 and 0.33 (the score runs from −1 to 1 and measures how cleanly groups separate). The one group that recurs across solutions holds 39 districts, 23 in Gujarat and 13 in Kerala, and is better read as a feature of those two states. Resampling districts moves 4.7 percent of them to another group on average, and measuring supply as an average standard score in place of an average rank leaves 88.9 percent in the same group.

The state comparisons use the 23 states with at least eight districts. The between-state share is the part of the spread across districts that the state a district is in explains; adjusting it for the number of states lowers it by at most 0.03. The within-state comparisons of the six groups include state fixed effects and let districts in the same state rise and fall together when measuring uncertainty.
