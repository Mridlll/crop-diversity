Markets and inputs

How rural markets and input shops line up with crop diversity

District by district, we set the share of villages with each kind of rural facility against two measures of crop diversity: the count of crops and the effective number of crops.

01 · What exists

The average district has a mandi in three villages out of a hundred and a weekly haat in fourteen.

Each dot is one village in a district of 100. Average share of villages across 606 districts. Low and high are the 10th and 90th percentiles, so one district in ten lies below low and one in ten above high. Mission Antyodaya 2019–20, via SHRUG 2.1.

In each of 606 districts across 30 states, we take the share of villages that report a facility in the 2019–20 Mission Antyodaya village survey. A district where 14 of every 100 villages have a weekly haat scores 14 percent.

The mandi, the regulated wholesale market where crops are auctioned under state rules, is the rarest of the ten facilities covered here. In the average district, 3.0 percent of villages have one, and 40 districts report none. Weekly haats are open-air markets that meet on a fixed day each week, and at 14.3 percent of villages they are close to five times as common. Producer organisations, registered groups of farmers that sell their members' produce and buy inputs for them jointly, are the most widespread facility. They are present in 23.1 percent of villages. Only five districts are without one.

A regular market is a permanent local market, which the survey records separately from mandis and weekly haats. Farmers buy seed at a government seed centre, and a soil testing centre tests their soil and advises on fertiliser. Custom hiring of machinery means a centre that rents out tractors and other equipment to farmers who own none. Food storage warehouses hold farm produce after harvest, and farm-gate processing covers primary processing such as milling or grading in the village itself.

Fertiliser shops are in 3.3 percent of villages in a district at the 10th percentile, the level a tenth of districts fall below, and in 39.8 percent at the 90th.

02 · How they group

Weekly haats are common in different districts from the other nine facilities.

Lines join facilities whose shares of villages correlate at 0.4 or more, and thicker lines mean higher correlation. Facilities common in the same districts are drawn near each other. The weekly haat is drawn closer to the others than its correlations would place it, so that it fits on the chart. Hover or tap a facility to read its correlations. 606 districts.

A correlation runs from minus one to one and measures how far two facilities tend to be common in the same districts. At zero, knowing one tells you nothing about the other. Across the 606 districts, nine of the facilities correlate with each other at between 0.24 and 0.83. Seed centres, soil testing, custom hiring of machinery, fertiliser shops, farm-gate processing and producer organisations all correlate with each other at 0.63 or more. Custom hiring of machinery and producer organisations are the closest pair, at 0.83.

Mandis, regular markets and weekly haats are where farmers sell, and together they make up the output markets. The weekly haat correlates with the other nine facilities at between −0.21 and +0.06, and its lowest values are against the other two output markets, regular markets at −0.21 and mandis at −0.15. Its correlations with the four input-supply facilities, fertiliser shops, seed centres, soil testing and custom hiring of machinery, run from −0.10 to −0.02.

03 · Where weekly haats are

Weekly haats are most common across eastern and central India and the northeast.

Share of villages with each facility, by district. The top and bottom 2 percent of districts take the end colours of the scale. Hatched districts have no facility record. State averages in the text are across the 606 districts used throughout. Mission Antyodaya 2019–20.

Chhattisgarh has the highest state average, with a weekly haat in 31 percent of villages. Jharkhand follows at 28 percent and Bihar at 25 percent, then Meghalaya and Assam at 23 percent and West Bengal at 22 percent. Switch the map to fertiliser shops or producer organisations and the highest shares move to Kerala and Gujarat.

04 · Development and state

Nine of the ten facilities are more common in districts with more non-farm businesses.

with non-farm businesses with night lights

Correlation across 606 districts between each facility's share of villages and either night-light brightness from satellite, on a log scale, or non-farm businesses per 1,000 people.

Night lights are the brightness of a district seen from satellite at night, which tracks economic activity. The count of non-farm businesses covers shops and other enterprises outside farming, per 1,000 people, from the Economic Census. All facilities except the weekly haat correlate positively with non-farm businesses, with fertiliser shops at +0.35 and regular markets at +0.33. The weekly haat runs at −0.16, and its correlation with night lights is close to zero at +0.04.

Districts with many fertiliser shops also tend to be richer, so each comparison holds night lights and non-farm businesses constant, meaning it sets districts against others with similar values of both. The same goes for an average of paved road, public transport, bank and railway access, the share of sown area irrigated, average farm size, and the share of people from Scheduled Tribes (communities the Constitution lists for protection).

Each comparison also uses a state baseline, which compares each district only with districts in its own state, so a district in Bihar is only ever set against other districts in Bihar. That removes everything a state's districts share, such as policy, rainfall and the way records are kept, and keeps crop patterns common to a whole state from being counted as a link with weekly haats. Economists call it a state fixed effect.

05 · Counting crops two ways

A district can grow 20 crops and still have an effective number of crops close to five.

Illustration with invented shares. Effective number of crops = exp(Shannon index of land shares). Land shares are shares of gross cropped area, which counts a field twice if it carries two crops in a year. District averages, measured in each year and then averaged over 1997–98 to 2019–20: count of crops 21.2, effective number of crops 4.9.

The count of crops is how many different crops a district grows at all, and the average district grows 21.2. The effective number of crops weighs each crop by how much land it covers. It equals the number of equally sized crops that would give the same spread of land. A district split evenly across 20 crops scores 20, and the score falls as any one crop takes a larger part of the land. The average district's effective number of crops is 4.9.

The example district here grows 20 crops, with 62 percent of its land under one cereal, and its effective number of crops is 4.9. Adding a twenty-first crop on one percent of the land raises the count by one and the effective number of crops to 5.1. Moving ten percent of the land from the cereal to the next four largest crops leaves the count at 20 and lifts the effective number of crops to 6.0.

06 · District by district

Chhattisgarh and Assam grow close to 30 crops per district, with an effective number of crops near three.

Count of crops and effective number of crops are district averages over 1997–98 to 2019–20. Facilities are shares of villages, Mission Antyodaya 2019–20. The top and bottom 2 percent of districts take the end colours of the scale. White lines are state borders. Hatched districts are outside the 606. Hover or tap a district for its value.

The map opens on the count of crops. Among the 23 states with at least eight districts, Chhattisgarh has the highest average, at 31.6 crops per district, with Karnataka at 29.7 and Assam at 29.0. Punjab is lowest, and its districts grow 9.1 crops on average.

Switch to the effective number of crops and the brightest districts move south. Karnataka leads at 8.0, followed by Tamil Nadu at 7.0 and Andhra Pradesh at 6.9, and Odisha is lowest at 2.5. Chhattisgarh drops to 3.1 and Assam to 3.2. Rice is the largest crop by area in every district of both states and covers about three quarters of the land, so the many other crops there share what is left.

Among the facility layers, fertiliser shops and producer organisations are far more common in Kerala, where they are in 83 and 86 percent of villages, and in Gujarat, at 55 and 66 percent, than anywhere else.

07 · States

About half the spread in the effective number of crops across districts lies between states.

Each dot is a district and each white tick a state average. The dashed line is the average across all 580 districts in the 23 states with at least eight districts. The bar is the share of the spread across districts that lies between states.

Karnataka's districts average 8.0 on the effective number of crops and Odisha's 2.5, and inside Karnataka alone they still run from 3.8 to 14.3. To split the spread, picture every district given its state's average. The spread that would remain is the part between states, and the bar under the chart gives it as a share of the total. For the effective number of crops that share is 49 percent. Switch to the count of crops and it rises to 82 percent, so a district's state goes most of the way to predicting how many crops it grows.

Facilities divide along state lines too. Between 64 and 79 percent of the spread in fertiliser shops, producer organisations, custom hiring of machinery, farm-gate processing, regular markets and seed centres lies between states. A comparison across all districts at once would mostly be a comparison of states, which is why the comparisons that follow set each district against others in its own state.

08 · Six groups of district

The best-supplied districts have a higher effective number of crops, a gap that disappears among districts of the same state.

Shading: a district's supply of the nine facilities other than the weekly haat, as its average percentile rank across them, cut into thirds. Hatching: share of villages with a weekly haat above the median of 12.6 percent. On each card the white dot is the group's average over 1997–98 to 2019–20 and the faint ticks mark the other five groups. Select a card to show its group alone.

The map sorts districts on two scales at once. The shade of violet gives a district's supply of the nine facilities that rise and fall together, cut into thirds from the least to the best supplied. Districts where more than 12.6 percent of villages have a weekly haat, the median, carry hatching on top. Because weekly haats bear no relation to the other facilities (a correlation of 0.02), haat-rich districts turn up in every third. Districts do not fall into natural clusters on these facilities, so the six groups are bands along the two scales.

Supply climbs steeply across the thirds. A fertiliser shop is in 5 to 8 percent of villages in a bottom-third district and in 31 to 33 percent in the top third, and producer organisations go from 9 to 11 percent up to 39 to 41 percent. Over the same thirds the effective number of crops rises from about 4.4 to about 5.6, while cereals' share of the land falls from 75 to 56 percent.

That gap goes with the states the districts sit in. Among districts of the same state, the thirds differ in the effective number of crops and in cereal share by amounts well within chance, with every p value above 0.2. A p value is the chance of seeing a gap at least this large if there were none, and below 0.05 is the usual bar. The state a district sits in accounts for 49 percent of the spread in the effective number of crops, close to ten times the 5 percent that goes with its group.

The weekly haat split holds up better on the count of crops. Within each third the haat-rich half grows 2.4 to 3.5 more crops and spreads its land less evenly. Evenness, the effective number of crops divided by the count, is 0.22 to 0.25 there and 0.27 to 0.29 in the other half. Inside states the haat-rich half still grows 1.4 more crops (p = 0.002). Its effective number of crops is 0.21 higher, at p = 0.43.

09 · The result

Weekly haats and producer organisations each go with a different measure of crop diversity.

Count of crops

Effective number of crops

One comparison per facility, with the state baseline and development, irrigation, farm size and Scheduled Tribe share held constant. Each figure is the change if every village had the facility, against none. Filled dots: p < 0.05. Lines show the range the true value very likely falls in (approximate 95 percent interval). 606 districts.

Each dot comes from a separate statistical comparison that looks at one facility at a time, with the state baseline and the development measures above held constant. The number plotted, called a coefficient, is the change in crop diversity as a facility's share of villages goes from none to all, a far wider gap than separates most districts. A more realistic step is one standard deviation, the typical distance of a district from the average. For weekly haats that is 11.7 percent of villages.

On the count of crops, the weekly haat has the largest coefficient, +7.33. A rise of one standard deviation in the share of villages with a weekly haat goes with about 0.9 more crops, against an average of 21.2. On the effective number of crops the weekly haat coefficient falls to +0.49, at p = 0.56. A p value below 0.05, which we call the five percent level, is the usual bar for a clear link. A link to the count of crops with almost none to the effective number of crops is the pattern you would expect if the extra crops sit on small plots while one staple still covers most of the land. A third measure, evenness, is the effective number of crops divided by the count of crops. It averages 0.254 and is 0.071 lower where every village has a weekly haat, in a comparison that includes all three output markets together (p = 0.055).

Producer organisations show the reverse pattern across the two measures. They are the only facility with a clear positive link to the effective number of crops, +1.95 at p = 0.032, or about 0.4 more for one standard deviation more villages with one. Their coefficient on the count of crops is +2.39 at p = 0.19, too imprecise to separate from zero. Seed centres also go with a higher count of crops, as do food storage warehouses and farm-gate processing, all with weaker evidence than the weekly haat.

10 · How firm the weekly haat result is

The weekly haat link to the count of crops stays between +6.4 and +8.0 across six adjusted versions of the comparison.

Weekly haat coefficient on the count of crops: the change if every village had one, against none. All p < 0.0001. 606 districts except where shown.

Before any adjustment, the weekly haat coefficient is +16.99. Adding the state baseline cuts it to +7.18, so most of the raw link came from where weekly haats are, in eastern and central India. Adding irrigation and then the full set of development measures leaves it close to +7. Leaving out the 64 districts that share their 2011 district with another district gives +8.02. The figure is +6.44 once the 30 districts without a weekly haat are set aside, and +6.87 with all nine other facilities in the same comparison.

All versions have p < 0.0001. Measuring weekly haats per 100,000 people, in place of the share of villages, gives 0.054 more crops for each extra weekly haat per 100,000 people, at p = 0.008.

11 · Which crops

Districts with more fertiliser shops put less of their land under pulses.

Change in pulse share, percentage points, if every village had the facility

Share of cropped area, with all years from 1997–98 to 2019–20 added together. One comparison per facility. One standard deviation of the share of villages with a fertiliser shop is 17.6 percent of villages. Filled dots: p < 0.05. q values: Benjamini-Hochberg across ten crop categories, with facilities compared together by group.

Facilities also line up with which crops take a district's land. In the average district, pulses cover 10.1 percent of cropped area and cereals 66 percent. Fertiliser shops go with a pulse share 17.4 percentage points lower if every village had one, which comes to about 3.1 points for one standard deviation more villages with a fertiliser shop. In the grid, three of the ten pulse squares go to other crops. Food storage warehouses, at −17.2, and regular markets, at −9.4, also go with less land under pulses.

With many facilities and ten crop categories tested, some links will look significant by chance. A standard correction for testing many links at once (Benjamini-Hochberg) raises the bar to allow for that. Its q value plays the part of p, and below 0.05 still counts as clear. The fertiliser shop result passes it easily when the four input-supply facilities are in the same comparison, at −19.0 points with q = 0.0004. Regular markets on pulses (q = 0.006) and weekly haats on the share of land under sugar crops, +11.1 points (q = 0.010), also pass.

Cereal shares move in mixed directions across facilities, and only fertiliser shops clear the five percent level, at −19.8 points. With food storage warehouses and farm-gate processing in the same comparison, producer organisations go with a cereal share 22.2 points lower, with more land under vegetables (+5.7) and under fruit (+3.2).

12 · Mandis and cereals

Districts with more mandis have a slightly smaller cereal share, a result just short of the five percent level (p = 0.054).

One comparison per facility, with the state baseline and all measures held constant. Change in cereal share of cropped area, in percentage points, if every village had the facility. Filled dots: p < 0.05. Lines show the range the true value very likely falls in (approximate 95 percent interval). 606 districts.

Districts with more mandis are often expected to grow more cereals. Across districts, the cereal share is 23.5 percentage points lower where every village has a mandi, at p = 0.054. One standard deviation of the share of villages with a mandi is 5.2 percent of villages, which puts the realistic gap at about 1.2 points of cropped area against an average cereal share of 66 percent.

13 · Markets and irrigation

Where a larger share of villages has a mandi, the effective number of crops peaks at a lower irrigation share and bends down less sharply.

one standard deviation below the average district one standard deviation above

Curves start at zero for an unirrigated district, and only their shape is estimated. Low and high are one standard deviation below and above each measure's mean. Post-harvest: food storage warehouses and farm-gate processing. 606 districts, with the state baseline and all measures held constant.

Across districts, the effective number of crops climbs with irrigation up to somewhere between a quarter and a half of sown area irrigated, and falls after that. A natural guess is that more markets and input supply speed the move into a few irrigated staples, which would make that fall steeper where they are common.

Each pair of lines shows the curve for districts one standard deviation below and above the average on each of the five tabs. The bend is a number for how sharply the curve turns down at high irrigation, and the more negative it is, the sharper the fall. For mandis, the bend is −8.98 in districts with a low share of villages with a mandi and −3.74 in districts with a high share, a difference significant at p = 0.033. The curve peaks at a lower irrigation share where more villages have a mandi, about 15 percent against 36 percent where fewer do. Because it barely rises before turning, the high-share curve runs below the low-share curve at every irrigation level, and both end about 2.5 below an unirrigated district at full irrigation.

On all five tabs the high-share curve bends down less sharply, with p between 0.056 and 0.143 for fertiliser shops and the three group averages (output markets, input supply and post-harvest). Each group average combines its facilities' shares of villages on a common scale.

14 · Reading these results

These are associations between facilities recorded in 2019–20 and long-run cropping.

Cropping: district crop area, 1997–98 to 2019–20. Facilities: Mission Antyodaya 2019–20.

Facilities may well be built where there is already a surplus to trade, so a district's crops can shape its markets as much as the other way round. The timing makes the direction harder to read, because facilities are recorded in 2019–20 while the cropping measures average every year from 1997–98 to 2019–20. Whether a new weekly haat or producer organisation would change what farmers grow is a question these comparisons cannot answer.

How this was estimated

The sample is 606 districts in 30 states that have both a long-run crop record and a 2019–20 facility record. Each facility is the share of a district's villages reporting it. Each group average puts its facilities' shares on a common scale (distance from the mean in standard deviations) and averages them. Output markets combine mandis, regular markets and weekly haats, and input supply combines fertiliser shops, seed centres, soil testing and custom hiring of machinery. The post-harvest average covers food storage warehouses and farm-gate processing, and the connectivity average covers paved roads, public transport, banks and railways.

The outcomes are the count of crops (mean 21.2), the effective number of crops (the exponential of the Shannon index of land shares, mean 4.9), evenness (the effective number of crops divided by the count, mean 0.254), and each crop category's share of gross cropped area with all years from 1997–98 to 2019–20 added together (cereals 0.66, pulses 0.10). The diversity measures are computed within each year and then averaged across years. Each adjusted model controls for the share of sown area irrigated and its square, log mean holding size, the Scheduled Tribe population share, log night lights, non-farm businesses per 1,000 people, and the connectivity average. All of them also include the state baseline.

The uncertainty ranges allow some districts to be more scattered than others. Districts split from the same 2011 district are treated as one group when measuring uncertainty, because their figures are likely to rise and fall together. In this sample almost none of those districts has a partner from the same 2011 district, so the 606 districts form 605 groups. Leaving out all 64 districts that share their 2011 district with another district puts the weekly haat result at +8.02, across 542 districts.

The main results put one facility in each comparison, because the facilities overlap so much that putting them together blurs each one's estimate. Within the input-supply group, the variance inflation factor (how far a coefficient's uncertainty grows because that facility is predictable from the others) runs from 3.4 to 4.1. Facilities are also compared together in groups. The output markets form one group and the input-supply facilities another, and a third holds food storage warehouses, farm-gate processing and producer organisations. For each facility in those groups, q values apply a Benjamini-Hochberg correction across the ten crop categories.

In the irrigation comparison, the curve's shape is allowed to change with each facility's share of villages. The bend and peak are read at one standard deviation below and above the mean. Across versions of the model the peak falls between about a quarter and a half of sown area irrigated, and between 15 and 36 percent at high and low shares of villages with a mandi.

The six groups of district rest on two measures. Facility supply is a district's average percentile rank on the nine facilities other than the weekly haat, cut into thirds at 0.40 and 0.60. The weekly haat split is at the median share of villages, 0.126, and the two measures correlate at 0.017. A k-means search on the facility shares, for three to six groups under three scalings and once more without the weekly haat, gives silhouette scores between 0.15 and 0.38 (the score runs from −1 to 1 and measures how cleanly groups separate). The first principal component carries 59 percent of the variance and loads on every facility except the weekly haat, and the second, with 11 percent, loads mainly on the weekly haat. The one group that recurs across solutions holds 37 districts, 23 in Gujarat and 13 in Kerala, and is better read as a feature of those two states. Resampling districts moves 4.8 percent of them to another group on average, and measuring supply as an average standard score in place of an average rank leaves 86.8 percent in the same group.

The state comparisons use the 23 states with at least eight districts, 580 districts in all. The between-state share is the part of the spread across districts that the state a district is in explains. Adjusting it for the number of states lowers it by at most 0.02, and computing it over all 30 states changes it by at most 0.04. The within-state tests on the six groups include the state baseline and measure uncertainty allowing districts in the same state to rise and fall together.
