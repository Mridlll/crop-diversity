Markets and inputs

How rural markets and input shops line up with crop diversity

Where every rural resident lives near a weekly haat, a district grows roughly four to six more crops than where no one does, depending on whether district size is held constant, and most of those crops sit on slivers of land. Most other facilities follow state lines.

01 · What exists

In the average district, one rural resident in 28 lives in a village with a mandi, and one in five in a village with a weekly haat.

Each dot is one in a hundred of a district's rural people. Low and high are the 10th and 90th percentiles of districts.

The mandi, the regulated wholesale market where crops are auctioned under state rules, is the rarest facility, and 40 districts have none. Weekly haats, open-air markets that meet on a fixed day, reach about five times as many people.

Farmer collectives reach the most people, 28 percent, because the village credit cooperative that gives crop loans counts as one alongside producer organisations.

02 · How they group

Weekly haats are common in different districts from the other nine facilities.

Lines join facilities whose shares correlate across districts at 0.4 or more. Hover or tap a facility to read its correlations.

Although mandis, regular markets and weekly haats are all places where farmers sell, the weekly haat runs against the other two, at −0.34 with regular markets and −0.22 with mandis. Input suppliers, custom hiring and farmer collectives cluster most tightly.

Part of that gap comes from the survey taking one market per village. In the 2011 census, where a village can have both, haat reach and other-market reach go together (+0.42).

03 · Where weekly haats are

Weekly haats reach the most people across eastern and central India and the northeast.

Share of rural people living in a village with each facility. The top and bottom 2 percent of districts take the end colours of the scale, and hatched districts have no facility record.

In Chhattisgarh 44 percent of rural people live in a village with a weekly haat, and in Jharkhand 35 percent. Fertiliser shops and farmer collectives reach the most people in Kerala and Gujarat.

04 · Development and state

Weekly haats are the one facility that is scarcer in districts with more non-farm businesses.

with non-farm businesses with night lights

Correlation across districts between each facility's share and either night-light brightness seen from satellite, on a log scale, or non-farm businesses per 1,000 people.

Custom hiring, fertiliser shops, regular markets and farmer collectives are all more common where there are more non-farm businesses, whereas the weekly haat runs the other way, at −0.18.

Since richer districts have more of nearly everything, the cropping links further down compare districts within a state that have similar development, irrigation and farm size.

05 · Counting crops two ways

A district can grow 20 crops and still have an effective number of crops close to five.

Illustration with invented shares. Land shares are shares of gross cropped area, which counts a field twice if it carries two crops in a year.

The average district grows 21.4 crops, yet its effective number of crops, which weighs each crop by its land, is only 4.9.

In the example a twenty-first crop on one percent of the land lifts the effective number only from 4.9 to 5.1, whereas moving a tenth of the cereal land to four other crops takes it to 6.0.

06 · District by district

Chhattisgarh and Assam grow close to 30 crops per district, with an effective number of crops near three.

Crop measures are district averages over 1998–99 to 2019–20, facilities are shares of rural people with one in the village, and hatched districts have no usable record. Hover or tap a district for its value.

Among the larger states Chhattisgarh grows the most crops, 31.9 per district, against 9.1 in Punjab. On the effective number Karnataka leads at 8.1 and Odisha trails at 2.4.

Chhattisgarh and Assam fall to about three, because rice covers roughly three quarters of the land in every one of their districts and leaves the many other crops to share the rest.

07 · States

About half the spread in the effective number of crops across districts lies between states.

Each dot is a district and each white tick a state average. The dashed line is the average across districts. The bar is the share of the spread across districts that lies between states.

If every district took its state's average, it would keep 49 percent of the spread in the effective number of crops and 82 percent of the spread in the count, so a district's state goes most of the way to predicting how many crops it grows.

Most facilities divide along state lines even more sharply, with 60 to 77 percent of their spread lying between states.

08 · Six groups of district

The best-supplied districts have a higher effective number of crops, a gap that mostly comes from the states they are in.

Shading: supply of the nine facilities other than the weekly haat, in thirds. Hatching: more than the median 17.9 percent of rural people live near a weekly haat. Select a card to show its group alone.

A fertiliser shop reaches 11 to 13 percent of rural people in a bottom-third district and 40 to 45 percent in the top third. Over the same climb the effective number of crops rises from about 4.3 to between 5.4 and 6.4, yet most of that gap belongs to the states the districts sit in.

Weekly haats cut across all three thirds. Within state the haat-rich half grows 1.1 more crops (p = 0.006) while its effective number barely moves.

09 · The result

Districts where more people live near a weekly haat grow more crops without spreading their land more evenly among them.

Count of crops

Effective number of crops

Change from no rural resident to all of them having the facility in the village, within state and with development, irrigation, farm size and Scheduled Tribe share held constant. Filled dots have p < 0.05; lines are 95 percent intervals.

On the count of crops the weekly haat has the largest link, +6.28, so a district one standard deviation above average, with 15 percent more of its rural people near a haat, grows about 0.9 more crops against an average of 21.4. Its link to the effective number, +0.55, is not statistically significant, which is what you would expect if the extra crops sit on small plots while one staple still covers most of the land.

Farmer collectives go with more of both, +3.38 crops and +1.89 on the effective number, although the second falls short of significance once districts in a state are allowed to move together (p = 0.17). Since village credit cooperatives make up most of the measure, it says little about producer organisations as such.

10 · How many more crops

Haat districts grow roughly four to six more small crops, the lower figure once district size is held constant.

Change in the count of crops from no rural resident to all of them living in a village with a weekly haat. Hover or tap a dot for detail.

Within state, a district where everyone lives near a weekly haat grows about six more crops than one where no one does, and one standard deviation more haat reach adds 0.6 to 0.9 crops. Since larger districts record more crop names and haat districts tend to be larger, holding each district's cropped area constant brings the figure down to +3.76 (p = 0.05).

Cropped area may itself grow where markets are, so +3.76 is better read as a floor than as the answer.

11 · What the extra crops are

Most of the extra crops in haat-rich districts each cover less than a thousandth of the cropped area.

Within state, with all measures held constant. Filled dots stay below p = 0.05 when districts in the same state are allowed to move together. Crop by crop: the change in the share of years from 1998–99 to 2019–20 in which a district grows the crop.

Counting only crops that hold at least 0.1 percent of a district's cropped area, the haat link falls to +2.46, which is not statistically significant, and above 0.5 percent it disappears. Each extra crop takes a sliver of the district's land, which suits a market where a farmer can bring a basket on the weekly day.

Haat-rich districts grow sunflower, safflower and linseed in more of their years, along with turmeric, coriander, onion and several pulses. In the 2011 census, which records a haat wherever there is one, haat reach also goes with a higher effective number of crops (+1.86, about 0.37 for one standard deviation).

12 · Which crops

Districts with more fertiliser shops put less of their land under pulses.

Change in pulse share, percentage points, if every rural resident had the facility in the village

Share of cropped area over 1998–99 to 2019–20. One standard deviation of fertiliser-shop reach is 19.2 percent of rural people, and filled dots have p < 0.05.

Pulses cover 10.2 percent of cropped area in the average district, and one standard deviation more fertiliser-shop reach goes with a pulse share 2.3 points lower, close to a quarter of that land. Weekly haats go with 10.1 points more land under sugar crops, a link that rests mostly on the main cane states.

The fertiliser-shop links most likely run from crops to shops, because a dealer opens where farmers already buy fertiliser in quantity. Cotton is among the most input-hungry crops in India, so districts given over to it draw shops whether or not the shops changed anything.

13 · Mandis and cereals

Mandis show no clear link to how much of a district's land goes to cereals.

Change in cereal share of cropped area, in percentage points, from no rural resident to all of them having the facility in the village, within state with all measures held constant. Filled dots have p < 0.05; lines are 95 percent intervals.

Mandis, where the state buys grain at support prices, are often expected to pull districts toward cereals. Yet where everyone lives near one the cereal share is 15.3 points lower, a difference that is not statistically significant, and one standard deviation of mandi reach would move it by less than a point.

14 · Reading these results

These are associations between facilities recorded in 2019–20 and long-run cropping.

Cropping: district crop area, 1998–99 to 2019–20. Facilities: Mission Antyodaya 2019–20.

Facilities may well be built where there is already a surplus to trade, so a district's crops can shape its markets as much as the other way round. Whether a new weekly haat or farmer collective would change what farmers grow remains open.

Between 1991 and 2011 the share of rural people near a weekly market doubled in the average district, from 13 to 25 percent, yet districts where it rose more gained only 0.15 more crops for a 15-point rise (95 percent interval −0.19 to +0.48), too uncertain to confirm the link across districts or to rule it out.

How this was estimated

The sample is 606 districts in 30 states with both a long-run crop record and a 2019–20 facility record. Each facility is the share of a district's rural population, counted in the 2011 census, that lives in a village reporting the facility in the Mission Antyodaya 2019–20 survey. Across districts these population shares correlate with plain village shares at 0.92 to 0.97, and weighting by the survey's own 2019–20 population gives shares that correlate with village shares just as closely. Each group average puts its facilities' shares on a common scale (distance from the mean in standard deviations) and averages them. Output markets combine mandis, regular markets and weekly haats, and input supply combines fertiliser shops, seed centres, soil testing and custom hiring of machinery. The post-harvest average covers food storage warehouses and farm-gate processing, and the connectivity average covers paved roads, public transport, banks and railways, all on the same population basis.

A regular market is a permanent local market, recorded apart from mandis and weekly haats.

The outcomes are the count of crops (mean 21.4), the effective number of crops (mean 4.9) and evenness (mean 0.250), all defined on the Measurement page, and each crop category's share of gross cropped area with all years from 1998–99 to 2019–20 added together (cereals 0.66, pulses 0.10). The diversity measures are computed within each year and then averaged across years. Each adjusted model controls for the share of sown area irrigated and its square, log mean holding size, the Scheduled Tribe population share, log night lights, non-farm businesses per 1,000 people, and the connectivity average, with state fixed effects.

The uncertainty ranges allow some districts to be more scattered than others, and districts split from the same 2011 district are treated as one group because their figures are likely to rise and fall together. Leaving out every district that shares its 2011 district with another puts the weekly haat result at +6.91.

The main results put one facility in each comparison, because the facilities overlap so much that putting them together blurs each one's estimate. Facilities are also compared together in groups: the output markets, the input-supply facilities, and food storage warehouses, farm-gate processing and farmer collectives. For each facility in those groups, q values apply a Benjamini-Hochberg correction across the ten crop categories.

Measured by the share of villages in place of the share of people, the weekly haat coefficient is +7.36 on the count of crops and +0.46 on the effective number, and farmer collectives are +1.92 on the effective number. On village shares mandis go with a cereal share 23.5 points lower (p = 0.054) and food storage warehouses with a pulse share 17.1 points lower (p = 0.009). Neither reaches p < 0.05 on population shares, and the fertiliser-shop link to cereals is clearer on population shares.

The farmer collective item is question 12 of the Mission Antyodaya 2019–20 village schedule, "Does the village has any Farmers Collective", answered as Farmers Produce Organization (FPOs), Primary Agriculture Cooperative Society (PACS), both, or none. A village counts as having one for any of the first three answers.

Letting the irrigation curve bend differently with each facility's reach gives a gentler fall at high irrigation where mandis reach more people: a curvature of −8.21 at one standard deviation below the mean and −4.06 at one above (p = 0.060 on population shares, 0.030 on village shares), which is weak evidence. No facility steepens the fall: fertiliser shops make no difference (p = 0.95) and the output-market, input-supply and post-harvest averages give p between 0.24 and 0.69.

The comparison over time uses the census village directories. The 1991 directory records a market and its day of the week, the 2001 directory records no market at all, and the 2011 directory records weekly haats and mandis or regular markets as separate items, so haats are compared between 1991 (a market held on a named weekday) and 2011 (a weekly haat). Daily markets in 1991 are a poor match for mandis and regular markets in 2011, whose reach is eight times larger, and enter only as a control. Agricultural credit societies are compared between 2001 and 2011. Each round is weighted by the same 2011 village populations, only villages reporting the item in both rounds enter, and a district needs three quarters of its rural people in such villages. The 1991 market record is missing or thin for Gujarat, Haryana, Maharashtra, Odisha, Assam and Jammu and Kashmir, which leaves 338 districts in 17 states. Cropping is averaged over 1998–99 to 2002–03 and 2009–10 to 2013–14, and irrigation change is the census share irrigated from 2001 to 2011. Uncertainty treats districts split from one 2011 district as a group, and a wild bootstrap by state gives the same reading.

Within state, the thirds of facility supply differ in the effective number of crops by amounts that are not statistically significant (p = 0.053), and state accounts for 49 percent of the spread against 9 percent for the six groups. Middle- and top-third districts put about 4 points less of their land under cereals than bottom-third ones (p = 0.001), though the top third is no lower than the middle. Within each third the haat-rich half grows 3.0 to 4.8 more crops.

Larger districts record more crop names, and haat-rich districts tend to be larger. With log district cropped area held constant the weekly haat link to the count of crops is +3.76 (p = 0.019 with districts in a state allowed to move together, 0.050 by wild bootstrap), and the link to the effective number stays near zero. Without that control the link stays between +5.2 and +6.9 across samples of districts and alongside the nine other facilities. Counted as weekly haats per 100,000 people, each extra haat goes with 0.055 more crops (p = 0.007). Turmeric, banana and coriander are among six crops the source stops reporting after 2019–20, and with all six left out the count link is +5.17.

In the 2011 census a village records a weekly haat whether or not it also has a mandi or regular market. Census haat reach goes with a higher effective number of crops (+1.86, p = 0.027 by wild bootstrap by state) in every period and with any one state left out, and with any added control it stays between +1.56 and +2.36. Its link to the count of crops above the 0.5 percent floor (+2.11) is weaker and falls short of significance in most shorter periods.

The pulse links hold after a Benjamini-Hochberg correction across crop categories: fertiliser shops at −14.4 points among the input-supply facilities (q = 0.003), regular markets at −8.2 points (q = 0.005), and weekly haats at +10.1 points of sugar crops (q = 0.003). Fertiliser shops also go with 22.3 points less land under cereals (q = 0.025) and 13.0 points more under fibre crops, mostly cotton (q = 0.025). The sugar link stays between +8.1 and +11.2 with any one state left out, and falls to +3.5 (p = 0.012) without Uttar Pradesh, Uttarakhand and Gujarat together.

The six groups of district rest on two measures. Facility supply is a district's average percentile rank on the nine facilities other than the weekly haat, cut into thirds at 0.40 and 0.59. The weekly haat split is at the median share, 0.179, and the two measures correlate at −0.06. Districts do not fall into natural clusters, so the six groups are cuts through a continuous spread. The one cluster that does stand out holds 39 districts, 23 in Gujarat and 13 in Kerala, and is better read as a feature of those two states. Measuring supply as an average standard score in place of an average rank leaves 88.9 percent of districts in the same group.

The state comparisons use the 23 states with at least eight districts. The between-state share is the part of the spread across districts that the state a district is in explains; adjusting it for the number of states lowers it by at most 0.03. The within-state comparisons of the six groups include state fixed effects and let districts in the same state rise and fall together when measuring uncertainty.
