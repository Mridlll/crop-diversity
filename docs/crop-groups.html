Crop groups

Where cropped land moved between crop groups over two decades

We split each district's gross cropped area into nine crop groups and follow their shares from 1998–99 to 2019–20, then look at the food security missions and at what a failed monsoon does to the next season.

01 · Where each group is grown

Millets lost ground in three districts out of four, most heavily across the Deccan.

Share is the group's share of gross cropped area in 2017–18 to 2019–20. Change is that share minus the share in 1998–99 to 2000–01, in percentage points. Districts on 2011 boundaries, so a district created later is shaded with the district it was carved from. The top and bottom 2 percent of districts take the end colours. Hatched districts lack a usable record in one of the two windows.

Gross cropped area counts a field once for every crop it carries in a year, so a field with a kharif and a rabi crop counts twice. We divide it among nine groups: rice, wheat, maize, millets, pulses, oilseeds, cotton, sugarcane and other crops. Millets here are jowar, bajra, ragi and the small millets, and maize is a group of its own. Each group's share is compared between 1998–99 to 2000–01 and 2017–18 to 2019–20 in 467 districts.

Across those districts millets fell from 13.5 to 7.9 percent of gross cropped area, a loss of about 7 million hectares, and the share fell in 357 of them. Almost all of the fall happened inside districts as farmers changed what they grew. Maharashtra carries a large part of it, and leaving the state out brings the national fall to about 3.5 points. Gujarat lost 9.4 points of millets and 8.3 of oilseeds over the same years as its cotton share rose 12.3 points.

The national oilseed share barely moved, at −0.7 points with a standard error of 0.7. In the typical district oilseeds lost 1.45 points (standard error 0.4), and 290 districts lost share against 177 that gained. The 13 districts of Andhra Pradesh lost 12.9 points of oilseeds between them, about a million hectares.

02 · The national mix

Millets gave up more cropped area than any other group gained.

Share of gross cropped area by year, summed over the 352 districts with a usable record in all 22 years. Every panel's vertical axis spans 8 percentage points, so slopes compare across panels. Under each name is the change between 1998–2000 and 2017–19 across 467 districts, with its standard error in brackets.

Maize rose 1.3 points (standard error 0.3) and cotton 1.7 points (0.5). Wheat rose about a point. Its end window leans on two very large Madhya Pradesh wheat areas in 2018–19 and 2019–20, and closing the comparison in 2016–18 puts the wheat gain at 0.95 points (0.29).

The national rice share fell 2.0 points because cropped area grew mostly in districts that grow little rice. That shift in weight accounts for −2.3 points, and the change within districts is +0.3. The typical district's rice share moved by −0.4 points (0.4), and rice area across the 467 districts went from 38.6 to 39.3 million hectares, a change too small to tell apart from zero.

Pulses gained 2.9 points (0.6), about 6.6 million hectares, and the gain is regional. Most of it is in Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh and Karnataka; without Rajasthan it halves to 1.3 points, and districts split almost evenly, 235 gaining and 230 losing. The western states start from a drought. June–September rainfall in 1999 and 2000 was 34 and 31 percent short in Rajasthan and 48 and 42 percent short in Gujarat.

Maize took over millet land in about 50 districts. Where millets lost 2 points or more, the land they gave up went to pulses (+4.7 points) and cotton (+4.1) at least as much as to maize (+3.2).

03 · Rice in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana

Telangana added about 0.4 million hectares of rice by 2016–18, almost all of it in the rabi season.

Between 1998–2000 and 2016–18, Telangana's rice area grew by 420 thousand hectares (standard error 146), 398 thousand of it rabi rice. The rice share of its gross cropped area rose 3.1 points (1.5), and rose in 7 of its 9 districts. Set against the rest of India, the gain in rice area holds clearly, at +0.35 in logs with p below 0.001. The matching gain in district rice share, +2.3 points, has p = 0.17.

A single season, 2019–20, added close to half a million hectares more. Counting it, the gain comes to about 0.9 million hectares and 7.8 points of share, 613 thousand hectares of it rabi. Over the same two decades Telangana's millet share fell about 12 points and its cotton share rose about 16.

In Andhra Pradesh rice area fell by 442 thousand hectares (143), mostly in the kharif. The Godavari and Krishna delta led the fall: West Godavari lost 16.0 points of rice share, Krishna 14.0, Prakasam 13.3 and Guntur 8.3. Gross cropped area in the state fell by 709 thousand hectares (278) at the same time, which leaves the state's rice share 2.7 points lower (2.0), within reach of no change at all.

04 · Missions and prices

Districts that joined the pulses mission in 2007 show no rise in pulse share against their 2002–07 level.

Difference between mission districts and other districts in the crop's share of gross cropped area, in percentage points, by year around entry in 2007–08, with 2006–07 set to zero. Bars are 95 percent intervals. Comparisons are within district and within state and year. The pulses comparison ends in 2009–10, when the mission reached every district in those states.

The National Food Security Mission began in 2007–08 in selected districts, with separate lists for rice, wheat and pulses. Pulse-mission districts were catching up with the rest on pulse share from 1997–98 until about 2002–03, and the climb stops there. Measured against 2002–07, entry brought no detectable change in pulse share, +0.36 points (standard error 0.35), or in pulse area, +0.9 percent (4.1). A rise of more than about one point, on a mean share of 18 percent, is ruled out.

The rice and wheat missions did not raise their own crop's share either. Rice share moved −0.15 points (0.48) and wheat share −0.47 (0.43). Giving mission districts their own linear trend puts the rice estimate at −1.3 points (0.4), so rice leans negative.

The Odisha Millets Mission started in 2017. In its first seven districts, ragi area went from 43 thousand hectares in 2016–17 to 34 thousand in 2019–20, continuing a slide from 66 thousand in 2010. Districts outside the mission grow almost no millets, which leaves nothing to compare against and no causal estimate.

Minimum support prices rose sharply in 2018–19, most for millets and cotton. A district's exposure is its earlier crop mix weighted by each crop's price increase. More exposed districts did not move area toward the crops whose price rose most. No group's response survives a correction for testing nine groups, and reshuffling the price increases across groups gives p = 0.60.

05 · Rainfall shocks

After a monsoon 20 percent or more below normal, the following rabi is about 6 percent smaller.

Change in area against a year with June–September rainfall within 10 percent of the district's own 1997–2019 average, with 95 percent intervals. Comparisons are within district and within state and year. Rabi is matched to the monsoon just before it, and the millet panel to the kharif a year after the monsoon.

Each district's June–September rainfall is set against its own average. After a monsoon 20 percent or more short, rabi area comes in about 6 percent smaller, −0.063 in logs with a standard error of 0.013, and the loss grows with the shortfall. It falls on the less irrigated two-thirds of districts, about 10 percent in each of those thirds, against about 3 percent in the most irrigated third.

Farmers do not plant more rabi millet after a dry monsoon. Millet area holds steady as the other rabi crops shrink around it, so millet's share rises by 0.2 to 0.4 points on a mean of 4.3.

Rabi rice gives way. Where it is grown, mostly in Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka and the north-east, a dry monsoon cuts rabi rice area by about a quarter, −0.255 in logs (0.063), and rice loses about 3.3 points of rabi share, some of it to pulses. In the least irrigated third of districts rabi pulse area falls about a fifth, −0.22 in logs (0.055), while wheat area there holds steady.

A wet monsoon leaves a smaller mark on the next kharif. In millet-growing districts, millet area is about 5 percent lower after a monsoon 20 percent or more above normal, with total kharif area unchanged. The matching fall in millet share is too weak to report as a finding, and it does not grow with the size of the surplus.

How the comparisons are made

Crop area comes from district agricultural statistics by season for 1997–98 to 2019–20. Districts are held on their 2011 boundaries, and a district created later is added back to the one it was carved from. The trend comparisons use the 467 districts with at least two usable years in each window. Andhra Pradesh and Telangana are separated by their 2011 districts. Season shares are used only where the season split holds: it does not for Odisha in 2001–02 to 2004–05, for Bihar rice in 1999–2001, or for Tamil Nadu rice before 2017–18, which is reported once a year.

Niger seed reported in Andhra Pradesh from 2015–16 to 2019–20 and moth reported in Gujarat in 1998–99 imply far more cropped area than those districts carry in any other year, and both are left out. Madhya Pradesh wheat in 2018–19 and 2019–20 is well above earlier years but agrees with the state's own production figures, so it stays in, with 2016–18 as the alternative end window.

The national change is the change in summed group area over summed gross cropped area, with a standard error from resampling districts 1,000 times. The typical district's change is the plain mean of district changes.

Guar is a legume grown mostly for gum and fodder, and coconut a plantation crop, so both sit with other crops. Barley and other cereals do too.

The missions are compared with a stacked event study. Each entry year is compared only with districts not yet in the mission, and every comparison holds district and state-year baselines, with uncertainty that lets a 2011 district's years move together. The pulses result depends on the baseline years. Against the single year before entry the change is −0.30 points (0.27); against 2004–06 it is +1.13 (0.39), because those two years dip. Dropping one state at a time keeps the change against 2003–07 between +0.13 and +0.56 points. From 2019–20 the Odisha statistics cover 39 crops in place of 13 or 14, adding jowar, bajra and the small millets in that year alone, so the ragi series is the only millet record that runs unbroken through the mission.

The price comparison takes each crop's 2018–19 increase in the minimum support price in excess of its 2013–17 average, weights it by the district's 2013–16 crop shares, and follows each group's share from 2008 to 2019. Taking 2019–20 on its own, only pulses move, +0.50 points (0.22), one result in nine.

Rainfall is the IMD 0.25 degree gridded daily series, averaged over each district. Rabi area is set against that crop year's monsoon. Each estimate holds district and state-year baselines and clusters on the 2011 district, 586 in all. District-years whose annual totals are unreliable are left out, as is 1997–98; the total rabi area estimate also leaves out milder jumps in cropped area. Reshuffling whole rainfall years 300 times gives p = 0.003 for total rabi area, and clustering on state gives p = 0.012. By irrigation third the rabi area change is −0.095 (0.035), −0.106 (0.022) and −0.026 (0.014) in logs. Rabi rice is grown in 49 districts, 22 in Andhra Pradesh, 9 in Karnataka, 16 in the north-east and 2 in Goa, and the estimate rests on the 45 of them with usable years. The least irrigated third includes many hill and north-eastern districts, where rabi wheat is mostly rainfed; without them the rabi pulse area change is −0.21 (0.084).

We also asked whether a change in one district's crop mix spreads to its neighbours. Within a state and year, adjacent districts' shares move together strongly in the same year, at 0.57 (0.04) for pulses, 0.53 (0.06) for millets and 0.70 (0.05) for oilseeds, and most of that reverses the following year. A district's change in pulse share follows its neighbours' change of the year before by about 0.05 to 0.07 per point, with p between 0.01 and 0.24 depending on how uncertainty is measured. Districts in the same state about 100 kilometres away, sharing no border, carry the same link, 0.057 (0.019) against 0.053 (0.034) for neighbours, which fits a shared regional pattern better than spread across borders. Millet changes show no positive link to neighbours' earlier changes. On a neighbour network where every link runs both ways, these data cannot establish spread from district to district, and cannot rule it out.
