Neighbours

Do districts crop like the districts next door?

Districts that share a border grow much the same crops, and in any one year their crop shares rise and fall together. Yet when a district changes course, its neighbours' moves of the year before predict the change little better than the moves of districts 100 kilometres away that share no border with it.

01 · Distance and the state line

Neighbours in the same state share about three-quarters of their crop mix, and a state line costs roughly as much likeness as 100 extra kilometres.

Likeness of two districts' crop mixes across nine crop groups, 100 for identical shares of cropped land and 0 for nothing in common, by distance between their centres. The two large dots are pairs that share a border. Hover or tap for each band.

Two adjacent districts in one state have 77 percent of their cropped land in common, against 67 across a state line, which is as alike as districts in one state are when they lie about 100 km further apart.

Borders often run where terrain and water change, and once those differences are held fixed the cost of the line roughly halves. Counted only on the eight crops every state records, a state line still costs 6 points of likeness.

02 · How alike is your neighbour

Where a district has neighbours in two states, the one least like it usually lies across the state line.

Share of cropped land in each crop group, 2015–16 to 2019–20. The thin white tick in each neighbour's bar is the chosen district's own share. Neighbours in another state are marked in magenta. Districts are on their 2011 boundaries.

Sirsa, in Haryana, shares 82 points of its mix with Fatehabad on the Haryana side but only 50 with Hanumangarh across the Rajasthan line, which gives a third of its land to crops outside the main groups and a sixth to pulses.

Of the 356 districts with neighbours in two states, 63 percent have their least similar neighbour across the state line, even though only 39 percent of their neighbours lie there.

03 · Which crops differ at the border

Rice and wheat make up the largest part of the gap at a state line, with oilseeds close behind.

Average share of cropped land in the border districts on each side, in percent, for borders with at least eight pairs of adjacent districts. Hover or tap a border for the gap within pairs and the part of it that geography predicts.

Rice and wheat account for about two-fifths of the likeness lost at a state line and oilseeds for about a quarter, although once distance, rainfall, terrain and irrigation are held fixed no single crop group's gap is statistically significant. Haryana's border districts put 35 percent of their land under wheat against 16 percent in the Rajasthan districts facing them, and geography predicts only 1.5 points of that gap.

Since rice and wheat are the crops state agencies buy at the support price, procurement is one candidate, but the states that buy most also built the canals, run their own seed and extension services and record crop area their own way.

04 · Borders over time

On average borders did not pull further apart over two decades, though a few moved a long way.

Gap in the crop's share of cropped land between paired border districts, first-named state minus second-named, in percentage points, by crop year. Hollow points are years in which fewer than half the pairs have a usable record. The dashed line marks 2014–15, when Telangana became a state. Hover or tap for each year.

Adjacent districts in one state went from a likeness of 78.8 to 76.0 over two decades and those across a state line from 67.6 to 65.3, so the gap hardly moved, and a widening of more than about 3 points can be ruled out.

Telangana's border districts moved far more into cotton than the Andhra Pradesh districts across the line, from 6 points above them to 21, though about four-fifths of that rise came before Telangana became a state and three districts a side are too few to tell it from chance. Punjab's rice lead over Haryana sat near 10 points throughout.

05 · How a crop spreads

New soybean land tended to appear next to districts that already grew it, a pattern that copying and state programmes would both draw.

Three in four districts that took up soybean between 2001 and 2016 bordered a district that already grew it, against 56 percent among similar districts in the same state, so suitable land alone does not explain where soybean went next. Chickpea is less clear, at 82 percent against 77.

Farmers copying the district next door would leave this trail on a map, yet so would a state programme working outward from its first districts, or a processing plant drawing supply from further afield.

06 · Neighbours or the region

Chickpea and soybean take-up leans toward same-state neighbours, although on so few take-ups that the lean is not statistically significant.

Set against districts about 100 km away, neighbours that already grow a crop predict take-up better only for chickpea surges and soybean, and only when they lie in the same state. The edge rests on a handful of take-ups and is not statistically significant for either crop, but if it is real it fits programmes and seed supply, which mostly stop at state lines, better than copying.

Cotton after Bt seed is the best-studied case of Indian farmers learning from one another inside villages, yet between districts a neighbour's cotton surge predicts a district's own surge no better than one 100 km away.

A district's crop shares move with its neighbours' in the same year and hardly at all in the years before or after, which is what shared weather, prices and programmes produce. One district following another would tilt the earlier years upward, and only pulses as a whole lean that way, weakly.

What the evidence can and cannot separate

Districts are held on their 2011 boundaries, with a district created later added back to the one it was carved from, and Telangana counted as its own state throughout. Crop area comes from district agricultural statistics for 1998–99 to 2019–20. A crop mix is each of nine groups' share of gross cropped area, which counts a field once for every crop it carries in a year. Likeness between two districts is 100 minus half the summed absolute difference between their nine shares, so it is the share of cropped land the two have in common. The early window is 1998–99 to 2002–03 and the late one 2015–16 to 2019–20, and district-years whose annual totals are unreliable are left out.

Two districts are neighbours when their 2011 boundaries touch, at a tolerance of 500 metres; there are 1,638 such pairs, with centres 79 km apart on average. Pairs across the Andhra Pradesh and Telangana line, a border only from 2014, are left out of the border comparisons and followed on their own.

The border comparison takes adjacent pairs in 2015–19 and estimates how much less alike a cross-border pair is, first holding distance fixed, then adding the gaps between the two districts in long-run rainfall and its variability, elevation, ruggedness and irrigated share, and finally comparing each cross-border pair with the average likeness inside its two states. The design borrows the contiguous county-pair comparison that Dube, Lester and Reich (2010) used for minimum wages across American state lines, in which adjacent places on either side of a border stand in for each other. Adjacent districts across a state line differ about twice as much in ruggedness as adjacent districts inside a state, and their irrigated shares differ by 23 points against 13. Uncertainty allows any two pairs that share a district to be correlated, and a more cautious version allows whole states to be. Measured geography leaves out soils and groundwater depth, so the part of the border gap left after geography may still hold some differences in land and water.

Likeness is also computed on the eight crops that every state in the border comparisons records: rice, wheat, maize, chickpea (gram), pigeon pea (tur), groundnut, rapeseed and mustard, and cotton, each as a share of the eight crops' combined area. Where one of the eight is missing from a state's record, it is also rare in the state across the line, except in Goa, and leaving Goa out changes nothing.

Change in diversity and in rice share over the two decades was shared by neighbours inside a state far more than across a state line. Moran's I, where 0 means no resemblance between neighbouring values, is 0.55 for diversity and 0.57 for rice inside states against 0.18 and 0.15 across them. Most of that contrast comes from whole states moving together, which a statewide change in how crops are recorded would also produce. Millets fell together on both sides of borders, at 0.60 inside and 0.62 across. Jharkhand's border districts fell 19 to 32 points below the Odisha districts facing them on rice in 2018 and 2019, a drop that rests on six pairs and two years and could come from how the record was kept.

The border-over-time comparison follows every adjacent pair each year from 1998–99 to 2019–20, holding fixed everything constant about a pair and every swing common to a year.

Take-up comes in two kinds. A district takes up a crop when its share crosses 5 percent of cropped land (2 percent for rabi maize) and stays above it; it surges when the share rises 5 points above its own 1998–2000 level and stays there. The yearly chance of either is compared across districts at risk, 2001–2019, within state and year, holding fixed the starting share and the five geography measures. The ring is the districts that share no border but lie at 0.75 to 1.5 times the district's average distance to its own neighbours, 2.4 districts on average and in any state. The benchmark for the maps asks how often a take-up would border an earlier grower if it fell instead on one of the five districts in the same state most like it in rainfall, rainfall variability, elevation, ruggedness, irrigated share and starting share. Matching on state alone puts that chance at 47 percent for soybean and 66 percent for chickpea, which flatters the neighbour pattern.

The state-line split of the take-up comparison uses districts that have neighbours on both sides of a state line, and sets same-state neighbours, other-state neighbours and the ring on each side against one another in a single estimate. Treating Telangana as part of Andhra Pradesh before 2014 gives the same answer. The food security mission's district lists for pulses vary within a state only in 2007–08 to 2009–10, since the pulses component then covered whole states; holding them fixed leaves the chickpea edge unchanged, so whatever programme lies behind it works at a finer grain than those lists.

The years-before-and-after chart regresses a district's yearly change in a crop's share on its neighbours' change and the ring's change in each year from three before to three after, all at once, with district and state-by-year fixed effects and rainfall held fixed. Holding the district's own change of the year before and after as well gives the same flat pattern for chickpea, soybean and cotton.

Neighbouring districts can look alike for four reasons, and these comparisons speak to each differently.

Shared land and climate account for most of the likeness in levels. Likeness falls smoothly with distance on both sides of state lines, rice and wheat belts run straight across them, and neighbours that differ more in irrigation, ruggedness or rainfall are less alike.

Shared weather and prices fit the year-to-year record. Neighbours move together strongly within a year, the links in the years before and after are small and alike, and districts 100 km away carry part of it.

State policy and state markets fit the border record: the cost of crossing a state line after geography, the weight of rice, wheat and oilseeds in it, changes in diversity and rice share that move statewide, and a neighbour edge in chickpea and soybean that stops at the state line. Procurement, input supply, extension and state seed and irrigation programmes are all candidates, and programmes aimed at clusters of districts inside a state, such as the pulses component of the food security mission, would produce the same within-state pattern. State departments also compile the crop statistics, so a reporting practice that differs by state looks like a border effect. Crop lists do change at state lines, by about five crops between adjacent districts, yet counting only the eight crops every state records leaves most of the gap in place.

Learning from neighbours would show up as neighbours predicting take-up better than equally distant non-neighbours, at a lag, and across state lines as well as within them, since farmers and traders cross them. No crop passes all three. Village studies of Bt cotton find farmers learning from one another (Maertens 2017; Stone 2007), as studies of the green revolution did for high-yielding seed (Foster and Rosenzweig 1995), and nothing here contradicts them inside a village. District shares are also too coarse to see copying between farmers inside a district or along a short stretch of border, and a shock that is sharper near a border than 100 km away would pass the ring comparison without any learning.

Strength of each result. The fall in likeness with distance and the raw cost of a state line hold under every measure of likeness, and made-up borders, from India cut at random into 25 regions, produce no gap like the real one. The figure of about 100 km has a 95 percent interval of roughly 45 to 170 km. The gap left after geography, 3.4 points, is the weakest number on the page: it is only borderline significant once whole states are allowed to be correlated, and it reads 1.9 to 2.6 points on a correlation of shares, without the other-crops group, or without Telangana and Andhra Pradesh. Set against each pair's own two states it reads 4.1 and stays between 2.6 and 5.0 with any one state left out. On the eight crops every state records the three figures read 6.1, 3.2 and 4.3 points. No single crop group and no single border carries a gap that is significant once geography is held fixed. The Moran's I across borders leaves out links between Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, which lay in one state until 2014; with them it reads 0.11 for diversity and 0.12 for rice. Changes on a single border rest on a handful of districts a side and are description only. No take-up comparison is significant once all fourteen kinds of take-up are read together, and the same-state edge for soybean, on seven take-ups, has p between 0.06 and 0.13 once districts in a state are allowed to move together.
