Neighbours

Do districts crop like the districts next door?

Districts that share a border grow much the same crops, and in any one year their crop shares rise and fall together. Yet when a district changes course, its neighbours' moves of the year before predict the change little better than the moves of districts 100 kilometres away that share no border with it.

01 · Distance and the state line

Neighbours in the same state share about three-quarters of their crop mix, and a state line costs roughly as much likeness as 100 extra kilometres.

Likeness of two districts' crop mixes, 100 for identical shares of cropped land across nine crop groups and 0 for nothing in common, averaged over all pairs of districts in 20 km bands of distance between their centres. The two large dots are pairs that share a border. Hover or tap for each band.

Two adjacent districts in one state have 77 percent of their cropped land in common, and the figure slips steadily as districts lie further apart. Across a state line, adjacent districts share 67, a level that districts in one state reach only when they lie about 100 km further apart.

Borders in India often run where terrain and water change, and once those differences are held fixed the cost of the line roughly halves. Nor is it a quirk of each state's crop list. Counted only on the eight crops every state records, from rice and wheat to groundnut and cotton, a state line still costs 6 points of likeness, about as much as 70 to 120 km of distance.

02 · How alike is your neighbour

Where a district has neighbours in two states, the one least like it usually lies across the state line.

Share of cropped land in each crop group, 2015–16 to 2019–20. The thin white tick in each neighbour's bar is the chosen district's own share. Neighbours in another state are marked in magenta. Districts are on their 2011 boundaries.

Sirsa, in Haryana, grows wheat and cotton with some rice. It shares 82 points of its mix with Fatehabad on the Haryana side and 66 to 78 with the Punjab districts to its north, which grow the same crops. Hanumangarh, across the Rajasthan line, shares only 50, since it gives a third of its land to crops outside the eight main groups and a sixth to pulses.

Sirsa is a common case. Of the 356 districts with neighbours in two states, 63 percent have their least similar neighbour across the state line, even though only 39 percent of their neighbours lie there.

03 · Which crops differ at the border

Rice and wheat make up the largest part of the gap at a state line, with oilseeds close behind.

Average share of cropped land in the border districts on each side, in percent, for borders with at least eight pairs of adjacent districts. Each border rests on 8 to 25 pairs, too few to test on its own. Hover or tap a row for the gap within pairs and the part of it that measured geography predicts.

Before geography is held fixed, the gap in rice share between neighbours is 4.3 points wider across a state line than inside a state, and the wheat gap 1.6 points wider, while pulses and millets differ by a point or less. With distance, rainfall, elevation, ruggedness and irrigation held fixed, the rice and wheat gaps shrink to about 2 and 1 points, and no crop group's gap is statistically significant on its own. Taken together, rice and wheat account for about two-fifths of the likeness lost at the line, oilseeds for about a quarter and crops outside the main groups for a sixth. Pulses and millets show no gap that can be detected, although their intervals are about as wide as rice's, so a small one cannot be ruled out. When each cross-border pair is set against how alike neighbours are inside its own two states, a border still costs about 3.5 to 4 points of likeness.

On a few borders the difference is plain to see. Haryana's border districts put 35 percent of their cropped land under wheat in 2015–19, against 16 percent in the Rajasthan districts facing them, and measured geography predicts only 1.5 points of the 14-point gap between paired districts. Paired with the Haryana districts next door, Punjab's border districts grow 11 points more rice, of which geography predicts 1.7. On the eastern side, Chhattisgarh's border districts grew 9 points more rice than Odisha's in 1998–2002 and about the same by 2015–19.

Since rice and wheat are the crops that state agencies buy at the support price, procurement is one candidate. The states that buy most also built the canals and tubewells, run their own seed and extension services, and record crop area in their own ways, and a comparison of districts cannot pull those apart. Pulses and millets differ little on average, although a few borders, such as Karnataka against Maharashtra, divide pulse land sharply.

04 · Borders over time

On average borders did not pull further apart over two decades, though a few moved a long way.

Gap in the crop's share of cropped land between paired border districts, first-named state minus second-named, in percentage points, by crop year. Hollow points are years in which fewer than half the pairs have a usable record. The dashed line marks 2014–15, when Telangana became a state. Hover or tap for each year.

Across all borders, adjacent districts in one state went from a likeness of 78.8 in 1998–99 to 76.0 in 2019–20, and adjacent districts in different states from 67.6 to 65.3. The gap between the two hardly moved. In 2013–19 it was 0.14 points narrower than in 1998–2002, and a widening of more than about 3 points can be ruled out.

Telangana's border districts moved far more into cotton than their neighbours. Their cotton share ran 6 points above the Andhra Pradesh districts across the line in 1998–2002 and 21 points above them in 2015–19, and most pairs on the Maharashtra and Karnataka borders moved the same way. About four-fifths of that rise came by 2010–13, while Telangana was still part of Andhra Pradesh and its cotton share as a whole was climbing from 16 percent to 35 percent, so the new state did not set it off. With three Telangana districts on each border, none of these changes can be told apart from chance. Telangana's rice gap with Andhra Pradesh closed over the same years, as its border districts moved towards the high rice shares of the Krishna and Godavari districts. Punjab's rice lead over the Haryana districts next door, by contrast, sat near 10 points from start to finish. Jharkhand's border districts stayed within 6 points of the Odisha districts facing them on rice from 2008 to 2016, then fell 19 to 32 points below them in 2018 and 2019. That fall rests on six pairs of districts and two years, and could as easily come from how the record was kept.

Change in diversity and in rice share over the two decades was shared by neighbours inside a state far more than by neighbours across a state line. Moran's I, a standard measure of how much neighbouring values resemble each other where 0 means no resemblance, is 0.55 for diversity and 0.57 for rice inside states, against 0.18 and 0.15 across them. Most of that contrast comes from whole states moving together, which a statewide change in how crops are recorded would also produce. Wheat, pulses, millets and oilseeds show no clear contrast, and millets fell together on both sides of borders, at 0.60 inside and 0.62 across, as a decline driven by climate and prices would.

05 · How a crop spreads

New soybean land tended to appear next to districts that already grew it, a pattern that copying and state programmes would both draw.

A district takes up soybean when the crop's share of its cropped land crosses 5 percent and stays there. Three in four districts that took it up between 2001 and 2016 bordered a district that already grew it. Among districts in the same state that resembled them in rainfall, terrain, irrigation and starting share, only 56 percent would have, so suitable land alone does not explain where soybean went next. Maize and cotton lean the same way, less strongly.

Chickpea is less clear. When districts first take it up, 82 percent sit next to an earlier grower, yet similar districts in the same state would do so 77 percent of the time. Surges, where a district already growing chickpea adds 5 points of cropped land to it, look patchier on the map, with the nearest earlier surge more than 150 km away a third of the time, although 64 percent still border one against 43 percent among similar districts.

Farmers copying the district next door would leave this trail on a map, yet so would a state programme working outward from its first districts, a soybean processing plant drawing supply from further afield, or a frontier of suitable soil.

06 · Neighbours or the region

Neighbours predict chickpea and soybean take-up only when they sit in the same state, which points to state programmes more than to farmers copying farmers.

Rise in a district's yearly chance of taking up the crop if every district in the set already grows it, in percentage points, with 95 percent intervals, within state and year. Hover or tap a row for same-state and other-state neighbours.

Set against districts about 100 km away that share no border, neighbours that already grow a crop predict take-up better only for chickpea surges and soybean, and only when they lie in the same state. Across a state line their pull on both crops is close to nothing. Farmers and traders cross state lines while programmes and seed supply mostly do not, so cluster demonstrations under a state's pulses programme fit this better than copying. The gap between the two kinds of neighbour is wide but rests on a handful of take-ups, and only for soybean is it statistically significant.

Cotton's rise after Bt seed reached the market in 2002 is the best-studied case of Indian farmers taking up a crop from one another. Village studies trace it to a few early adopters whom others watched (Maertens 2017) and to waves of seed fashion in Warangal (Stone 2007). Between districts there is no sign of it: a neighbour that has surged into cotton predicts a district's own surge no better than a district 100 km away. District totals cannot see learning inside a village, though, so what this questions is the idea that cotton moved across the map by example.

Year by year, a district's crop shares move with its neighbours' in the same year and hardly at all with their moves in the years before or after. For chickpea, soybean and cotton the years before look like the years after, which is what shared weather, prices and programmes produce. One district following another would tilt the left half of each chart upward. Only pulses as a whole lean that way, and the lean does not survive allowing for the seven crops checked.

What the evidence can and cannot separate

Districts are held on their 2011 boundaries, with a district created later added back to the one it was carved from, and Telangana counted as its own state throughout. Crop area comes from district agricultural statistics for 1998–99 to 2019–20. A crop mix is each of nine groups' share of gross cropped area, which counts a field once for every crop it carries in a year. Likeness between two districts is 100 minus half the summed absolute difference between their nine shares, so it is the share of cropped land the two have in common. The early window is 1998–99 to 2002–03 and the late one 2015–16 to 2019–20, and district-years whose annual totals are unreliable are left out.

Two districts are neighbours when their 2011 boundaries touch, at a tolerance of 500 metres; there are 1,638 such pairs, with centres 79 km apart on average. Pairs across the Andhra Pradesh and Telangana line, a border only from 2014, are left out of the border comparisons and followed on their own.

The border comparison takes adjacent pairs in 2015–19 and estimates how much less alike a cross-border pair is, first holding distance fixed, then adding the gaps between the two districts in long-run rainfall and its variability, elevation, ruggedness and irrigated share, and finally comparing each cross-border pair with the average likeness inside its two states. The design borrows the contiguous county-pair comparison that Dube, Lester and Reich (2010) used for minimum wages across American state lines, in which adjacent places on either side of a border stand in for each other. A placebo repeats the first step 500 times with India cut into 25 made-up regions. Adjacent districts across a state line differ about twice as much in ruggedness as adjacent districts inside a state, and their irrigated shares differ by 23 points against 13. Uncertainty allows any two pairs that share a district to be correlated, and a more cautious version allows whole states to be. Measured geography leaves out soils and groundwater depth, so the part of the border gap left after geography may still hold some differences in land and water.

To check that the border gap is not an artefact of each state's crop list, likeness is also computed on the eight crops that every state in the border comparisons records: rice, wheat, maize, chickpea (gram), pigeon pea (tur), groundnut, rapeseed and mustard, and cotton, each as a share of the eight crops' combined area. Where one of the eight is missing from a state's record, it is also rare in the state across the line, except in Goa, and leaving Goa out changes nothing.

The border-over-time comparison follows every adjacent pair each year from 1998–99 to 2019–20, holding fixed everything constant about a pair and every swing common to a year.

Take-up comes in two kinds. A district takes up a crop when its share crosses 5 percent of cropped land (2 percent for rabi maize) and stays above it; it surges when the share rises 5 points above its own 1998–2000 level and stays there. The yearly chance of either is compared across districts at risk, 2001–2019, within state and year, holding fixed the starting share and the five geography measures. The ring is the districts that share no border but lie at 0.75 to 1.5 times the district's average distance to its own neighbours, 2.4 districts on average and in any state. The benchmark for the maps asks how often a take-up would border an earlier grower if it fell instead on one of the five districts in the same state most like it in rainfall, rainfall variability, elevation, ruggedness, irrigated share and starting share. Matching on state alone puts that chance at 47 percent for soybean and 66 percent for chickpea, which flatters the neighbour pattern.

The state-line split of the take-up comparison uses districts that have neighbours on both sides of a state line, and sets same-state neighbours, other-state neighbours and the ring on each side against one another in a single estimate. Treating Telangana as part of Andhra Pradesh before 2014 gives the same answer. The food security mission's district lists for pulses vary within a state only in 2007–08 to 2009–10, since the pulses component then covered whole states; holding them fixed leaves the chickpea edge unchanged, so whatever programme lies behind it works at a finer grain than those lists.

The years-before-and-after chart regresses a district's yearly change in a crop's share on its neighbours' change and the ring's change in each year from three before to three after, all at once, with district and state-by-year fixed effects and rainfall held fixed. Holding the district's own change of the year before and after as well gives the same flat pattern for chickpea, soybean and cotton.

Neighbouring districts can look alike for four reasons, and these comparisons speak to each differently.

Shared land and climate account for most of the likeness in levels. Likeness falls smoothly with distance on both sides of state lines, rice and wheat belts run straight across them, and neighbours that differ more in irrigation, ruggedness or rainfall are less alike.

Shared weather and prices fit the year-to-year record. Neighbours move together strongly within a year, the links in the years before and after are small and alike, and districts 100 km away carry part of it.

State policy and state markets fit the border record: the cost of crossing a state line after geography, the weight of rice, wheat and oilseeds in it, changes in diversity and rice share that move statewide, and a neighbour edge in chickpea and soybean that stops at the state line. Procurement, input supply, extension and state seed and irrigation programmes are all candidates, and programmes aimed at clusters of districts inside a state, such as the pulses component of the food security mission, would produce the same within-state pattern. State departments also compile the crop statistics, so a reporting practice that differs by state looks like a border effect. Crop lists do change at state lines, by about five crops between adjacent districts, yet counting only the eight crops every state records leaves most of the gap in place.

Learning from neighbours would show up as neighbours predicting take-up better than equally distant non-neighbours, at a lag, and across state lines as well as within them, since farmers and traders cross them. No crop passes all three. Village studies of Bt cotton find farmers learning from one another (Maertens 2017; Stone 2007), as studies of the green revolution did for high-yielding seed (Foster and Rosenzweig 1995), and nothing here contradicts them inside a village. District shares are also too coarse to see copying between farmers inside a district or along a short stretch of border, and a shock that is sharper near a border than 100 km away would pass the ring comparison without any learning.

How firm each result is. The fall in likeness with distance and the raw cost of a state line hold under every measure of likeness, and the made-up regions produce no gap like the real one. The figure of about 100 km has a 95 percent interval of roughly 45 to 170 km. The gap left after geography, 3.4 points, is the weakest number on the page. Once whole states are allowed to be correlated it is only borderline significant, and it shrinks to between 1.9 and 2.6 points when likeness is measured as a correlation of shares, when the other-crops group is dropped, or when Telangana and Andhra Pradesh are left out. The version that sets each pair against its own two states, 4.1, holds up better, staying between 2.6 and 5.0 as states are dropped one at a time. On the eight crops every state records, the three versions read 6.1, 3.2 and 4.3 points. No single crop group and no single border carries a gap that is significant once geography is held fixed. The Moran's I across borders leaves out links between Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, which lay in one state until 2014; with them it reads 0.11 for diversity and 0.12 for rice. Changes on a single border rest on a handful of districts a side and are shown as description only. None of the take-up comparisons survives a correction for running 14 at once, and the same-state against other-state split rests on 7 to 17 take-ups per crop.
