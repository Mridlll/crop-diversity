Layer one

Counting crops, and weighting them

Diversity can be measured in crops. Doing so separates two things that a single score mixes together: how many crops a district grows, and how evenly it grows them.

Ecologists have largely agreed on how to measure diversity. Agricultural economics mostly has not adopted their approach. This page explains why that matters here, then lists four problems found in the original indices when they were rebuilt from the raw crop file.

The original measure was a composite. Shannon entropy, the Simpson index and crop richness were each rescaled from zero to one across the sample, then averaged with equal weights. This kind of composite is common and convenient. But each of the three steps carries an assumption.

The rescaling makes a district's score depend on other districts

Min-max normalisation subtracts the sample minimum and divides by the sample range. So a district's score depends on which other districts are in the file. Add a few very concentrated districts at the bottom of the range and every other score rises, even though no district changed what it grows.

To test this, the composite was recomputed on repeated random subsamples and each district was compared with its full-sample value. The result is below.

Figure 1

The composite moves when other districts move

Two hundred random eighty percent subsamples. Each observation is one subsample's mean absolute deviation from the full-sample score.

The shifts are small next to the index's standard deviation of roughly 0.15, so this is more a problem in principle than in practice. A measure still should not behave this way.

The equal weights have no stated reason

Nothing explains why richness, Shannon and Simpson should get a third each. Any other weighting would be just as easy to defend.

Two of the three components measure nearly the same thing

This is the bigger problem. Shannon and Simpson come from the same family of measures and differ only in how much weight they give rare categories. Across these districts they correlate at about 0.94. So the composite is roughly two parts evenness to one part count, not three equal parts. The weights actually applied are not even the equal weights that were chosen.

Consequence A single number that mixes how many crops a district grows with how evenly it spreads them cannot tell apart a district that adds a twentieth crop on half a hectare from one that rebalances its existing land. Later pages show that this difference separates two kinds of market infrastructure that otherwise look alike.

The alternative

Diversity as an effective number of crops

The standard fix is to convert each index into the number of equally common categories that would give the observed value. These are the Hill numbers. Each has an order q that sets how much weight rare categories get. They all share one unit, so they can be compared directly and read without rescaling.

The three orders used throughout this site, plus the ratio between the first two.
IndexDefinitionSensitivity to rare crops
D0crop richnessmaximal: a crop on one hectare counts as much as the staple
D1exp(Shannon)each crop weighted by its area share
D21 / Σp²minimal: dominated by the common crops
evennessD1 / D0how evenly area is spread, with the count divided out

D1 is the main measure. It gives the number of equal-area crops that would produce the diversity we observe. A district growing twenty crops with four fifths of its land under paddy has a D1 of about three or four, not twenty.

Two properties make these measures easier to use. The ordering D0 ≥ D1 ≥ D2 always holds, so it works as a check on the code. It passed for every district-year here. And because the unit is crops, a doubling of D1 means diversity doubled. That is not true of Shannon entropy in log units.

Figure 2

Where the districts sit on each order

Distribution across districts. As q rises, rare crops count for less and the distribution shifts toward the staples.

The gap between the D0 and D1 distributions shows crops that are grown, but on areas too small to change the district's overall cropping.

Construction

Four problems found in the original indices

The original indices were also checked against the raw area–production–yield file they were built from. One suspected problem turned out to be fine. Three were real.

Cleared: seasons are not double counted

In this data, the label Whole Year is sometimes a separate line for perennials and sometimes a total that already includes the seasonal rows. If it were usually a total, summing across seasons would double count every hectare. It is not. Well under one percent of Whole Year cells overlap with a named season for the same crop and district, and few of those look like real totals. Rebuilt national gross cropped area falls within the range of published figures, which rules out systematic duplication.

The last year is incomplete

The dataset is labelled as running to 2020–21, and the project described itself as covering twenty-four agricultural years. But the final year has only a few hundred rows across thirteen districts, against nineteen thousand rows and nearly two hundred million hectares the year before. It is a partial extract, not a full year of data.

Figure 3

Coverage collapses in the final year

Gross cropped area implied by the file, by agricultural year.

The usable range is 1997–98 to 2019–20, twenty-three years. The animated timeline elsewhere on this site still ends on the incomplete frame.

Richness was pooled across the whole period

This was the most important problem. The original code grouped by district but not by year. So richness counted every crop a district recorded at any point across two decades, not the crops it grows in a year. As a result, it runs about one and a half times as high as annual richness. It also rewards districts observed for longer, since more years of data give more chances to record a crop once.

Roughly a quarter of districts have fewer than twenty years of data. Pooled richness correlates with a district's number of observed years at about 0.55, against roughly 0.31 for annual richness. Since richness made up a third of the composite, part of the main index was measuring survey coverage.

Figure 4

Pooled richness tracks how long a district was observed

District means by number of years of available data. Only groups with at least five districts are shown.

pooled over the period measured per year, then averaged

If richness only reflected a district's farming, neither line would slope. The pooled line does.

Duplicate rows were summed

A small number of exact duplicates on district, year, season and crop survive the original code's cleaning, mostly in one oilseed in one state. A group-and-sum adds them together and inflates area for those cells. The effect is negligible nationally but still wrong, so the rebuild collapses them.

Consequences

What the corrections change

Rebuilding the indices year by year, dropping the incomplete final year and collapsing the duplicates leaves the broad picture in place. The corrected and original composites correlate at about 0.95, and the top state is unchanged. At district level, though, the agreement is weaker than the correlation suggests.

Figure 5

How far districts move when the construction is fixed

Absolute change in rank position out of roughly 725 districts.

The districts that move most recorded many crops across the period but grew few in any single year. Pooling had credited them with diversity they did not have in the field.

The bigger change is in the results. With the original pooled measure, crop richness showed no relationship with irrigation. With the corrected annual measure, it shows the same pattern as every other index. The construction error had hidden a real result rather than created a false one.
