Measurement

How crop diversity is measured

Each district's crop diversity is measured in units of crops. Every measure is computed within a single year and then averaged over 23 years.

606 districts30 states54 cropscropping 1997–98 to 2019–20

01 · Counting crops two ways

A district with twenty crops and four fifths of its land under rice has an effective number of crops below three.

Each plot is one percent of the district's gross cropped area, which counts a field twice if it carries two crops in a year. Effective number of crops = exp(Shannon index of land shares). Presets are invented, except the two named districts, which use their 2019–20 shares. Crops under half a percent of the land may get no plot of their own and still count in the measures.

The count of crops is how many different crops a district grows in a year. The effective number of crops weighs each crop by the land it covers. It equals the number of equally sized crops that would give the same spread of land. When every crop has the same share the two measures agree, and the effective number falls as any one crop takes more of the land.

Twenty crops with rice on 80 percent of the land give an effective number of crops of 2.97 at most, and less if the other nineteen are uneven. Bankura in West Bengal grew 23 crops in 2019–20 with rice on 78 percent of its land, and its effective number of crops was 2.4. Across the 606 districts the average count of crops is 21.2 and the average effective number of crops is 4.9.

Evenness is the effective number of crops divided by the count. It averages 0.254, so the typical district spreads its land about as evenly as a quarter of its crops sharing it equally would.

02 · Three weightings

The count of crops is always at least the effective number of crops, and the effective number is always at least the number of main crops.

D(q) = (Σ piq)1/(1−q), where pi is crop i's share of the land, with D1 taken as the limit exp(−Σ p ln p). The faint line is the previous example. The ordering held in all 14,089 district-years measured.

All three measures come from one formula with a single setting, called q, that controls how much the largest crops count. At q = 0 every crop counts once, whatever its area, which gives the count of crops, D0. At q = 1 each crop counts in proportion to its land, which gives the effective number of crops, D1. At q = 2 the dominant crops govern the result, which gives the number of main crops, D2. It counts crops with extra weight on those holding large shares of the land, so small crops barely register. Technically it is the inverse Simpson index, one divided by the chance that two plots picked at random carry the same crop.

All three are in units of crops, so they can be read side by side. A doubling of the effective number of crops means diversity doubled. The Shannon index itself is measured in logarithms, so a doubling of it has no such reading. The curve can only fall or stay flat as q rises, so a district's effective number of crops is never above its count of crops. Every district-year in the data follows this ordering.

03 · Reading the scale

The middle 80 percent of districts have an effective number of crops between 2.4 and 8.1.

Each dot is one of 606 districts, placed at the average of its yearly values over 1997–98 to 2019–20. The shaded band holds the middle 80 percent of districts. The marks at 1 and 2 stand for one crop on all the land and two crops on equal land. Lowest and highest are the districts at each end of the measure shown. Hover or tap a dot or a mark.

On the effective number of crops, 1 means a single crop on all of a district's land and 2 means two crops on equal halves. No district in the sample comes down to 1. The lowest is Bhadrak in Odisha at 1.38, where rice held about nine tenths of the land in an average year. One district in ten sits below 2.4, in the band marked low on the chart.

Half of the districts sit below 4.3. The average is higher at 4.9, since a smaller group of districts reaches far above the rest. Above 8.1 counts as high, and one district in ten gets there. Chamarajanagara in Karnataka is the highest at 14.3.

The count of crops runs along a longer stretch of the same axis. The median district grows 22.4 crops in a year and one in ten grows fewer than 11.1. Kargil in Ladakh has the shortest list at 3.7 crops a year, and at the far end Belagavi in Karnataka grows 36.6. The number of main crops is the lowest of the three, running from 1.13 in Bijapur, Chhattisgarh to 10.9 in Chamarajanagara with a median of 3.0.

04 · Across districts

Across districts the count of crops centres near 22 and the effective number of crops near 4.

One dot per district, the average of its yearly values over 1997–98 to 2019–20. White ticks are medians. One district in ten lies below and one in ten above: count of crops 11.1 and 29.3, effective number of crops 2.4 and 8.1. Hover, tap or search for a district to follow it across the strips. 606 districts.

In most districts a large count of crops sits on land held mostly by a few of them. The gap between the top strip and the middle one is made of crops that are grown on areas too small to change how the district's land is spread.

The effective number of crops and the number of main crops rise and fall together across districts, with a correlation of 0.97. The count of crops moves much more on its own, with a correlation of 0.50 with the effective number. Evenness is lower where more crops are grown, with a correlation of −0.38 with the count, because added crops usually arrive on small plots. For this reason the site reports the count of crops and the effective number of crops separately.

05 · Monoculture

In 119 of the 606 districts one crop holds at least two thirds of the land, and in 116 of them that crop is rice.

Top: invented districts, fifty plots per season, each plot an equal piece of land. Empty plots are fallow. Below: the largest crop's share is its average share of gross cropped area over the years a district reports, so land cropped in two seasons counts twice. Near-monoculture means the largest crop holds at least two thirds. Two crops hold the year when the two largest hold at least 80 percent and neither reaches two thirds. Few crops means fewer than 10 a year. 606 districts.

A district counts here as near-monoculture when its largest crop holds at least two thirds of its cropped land in an average year. That describes 119 districts. Rice is the crop in 116 of them, and they lie mostly in Odisha, Assam, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh and West Bengal. These districts still grow about 17 crops a year, and their median effective number of crops is 2.6.

Growing few crops is a separate condition. Only 24 districts grow fewer than 10 crops a year with no single crop on two thirds of the land and no two crops on four fifths. Most of them are in the hills of Arunachal Pradesh, Sikkim, Jammu and Kashmir and Himachal Pradesh, with four in Punjab. Their median effective number of crops is 3.4.

Another 88 districts give at least 80 percent of their land to two crops. Rice is the larger of the pair in 37 and wheat in 37, mostly in Uttar Pradesh, Bihar and Punjab. Across all 606 districts the largest crop's share has a correlation of −0.81 with the effective number of crops and −0.28 with the count of crops.

Among 555 districts observed in both 1998–99 to 2004–05 and 2013–14 to 2019–20, near-monoculture districts went from 133 to 114. The median share of the largest crop moved from 0.48 to 0.46, a change that chance could easily produce (p = 0.11).

06 · Cropping systems

Districts that grow rice twice a year have about two main crops, the same as districts with one rice crop and little after it.

A typical year in three systems, as a share of the year's cropped land

Map: each district's most frequent pattern across the years it reports, for all 725 districts with a crop record. The monsoon season covers kharif and the autumn and winter rice crops. The second season is rabi and summer together. Strips and figures are medians over the analysis districts in each system (117, 39 and 161). Hover or tap a district.

Each year a district's land is split by season. Rice then wheat means rice leads the monsoon season and wheat leads rabi, each on at least a fifth of the year's land. Rice twice a year means rice also leads rabi or summer on at least a tenth of the land. A district takes the pattern it shows most often, and the median district shows that pattern in 83 percent of its years. Rice then wheat covers 117 of the 606 districts, in Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Punjab and Haryana. Rice twice a year covers 39, in West Bengal, Assam, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and Odisha.

All measures on this site add a district's seasons into one year. A second rice crop raises rice's share without adding a crop, so districts growing rice twice end up with rice on 69 percent of their land and 2.1 main crops. The 161 districts that grow rice in the monsoon and little afterwards have 2.0. Wheat after rice counts as a second crop, which gives rice then wheat districts 2.9 main crops, with the two crops together on 80 percent of the land. A district with a low score on the diversity maps may grow rice once a year or twice, and the map above shows which.

07 · A rescaled score

A score rescaled between the sample's least and most diverse districts changes when other districts enter or leave the sample.

Toy: invented districts, scored on the effective number of crops rescaled from 0 to 1. Data: composite = mean of min-max rescaled count of crops, Shannon and Simpson, across 725 districts, recomputed on 200 random draws of 80 percent of them. Composite standard deviation 0.18.

A common way to combine diversity measures rescales each one so the least diverse district in the sample scores zero and the most diverse scores one, then averages them. A district's score then depends on which other districts are in the sample. Redrawing 80 percent of districts at random 200 times, with every district's crops held fixed, moved a district's composite score by 0.0024 on average and by 0.0156 at most. Both are small next to the composite's standard deviation, the typical distance of a district's score from the average, which is 0.18.

The equal weights also have no stated basis. Shannon and Simpson belong to the same family and correlate at 0.944 here, so a composite of them with the count gives what is close to a single land-weighted measure two thirds of the weight. The effective numbers need no rescaling. Each is a property of the district alone, measured in crops.

Invented shares as in scene 01, starting from 20 crops with one cereal on 62 percent. White plots are the land that changed. The composite change is rescaled against the range across the 606 districts. Market coefficients come from one comparison per facility, with the state baseline and development measures held constant. A single score that mixes the count of crops with how evenly land is spread cannot separate a district that adds a twenty-first crop on a small plot from one that moves land among the crops it already grows. The two measures keep them apart. In the example a crop added on one percent of the land raises the count by one and the effective number of crops by 0.2. Moving a tenth of the land from the main cereal to the next four crops raises the effective number by 1.1, with the count still at 20.

The market results follow this split. Going from no village with a weekly haat to every village having one goes with 7.33 more crops on the count of crops (p < 0.001) and 0.49 on the effective number of crops (p = 0.56). A p value is the chance of seeing a link at least this large if there were none, and below 0.05 is the usual bar. One standard deviation of the share of villages with a weekly haat is 11.7 percentage points, which goes with about 0.9 more crops. Producer organisations have a clear link to the effective number of crops (+1.95, p = 0.032) and an imprecise one to the count (+2.39, p = 0.19).

09 · Within a year

Counting every crop a district records across two decades gives about one and a half times the count in a typical year.

counted across all years counted each year, then averaged

Left: an invented district. Right: district means, grouped by years of data, for groups of at least five districts; dot size follows the number of districts. The groups shown hold 743 of 755 districts. Over all 755, the correlation with years of data is 0.545 for the count across all years and 0.309 for the yearly average.

A crop grown once in twenty-three years adds as much to a count taken across the whole period as a crop grown every year. Counting that way gives about one and a half times the count in a typical year. It also rises with the number of years a district has data for, since each extra year is another chance to record a rare crop. About a quarter of districts have fewer than twenty years of data. Every measure on this site is computed within each year and then averaged across the years a district has.

The choice changes rankings and results. Ranked by the count of crops, districts move a median of 74 places between the two ways of counting, and 277 of 725 move by more than 100. Most of those that move furthest recorded many crops across the period and grew few in any one year. Ranchi recorded 40 crops over 21 years and grew 12.6 in a typical year. Counted each year, the count of crops peaks at moderate irrigation like the other measures (p = 0.0001 for the peak). A count taken across all years shows no clear peak (p = 0.19).

How this was estimated

Source. District crop area, production and yield, 1997–98 to 2019–20, covering 54 crops, summed across seasons to gross cropped area per crop, district and year. The source also holds 2020–21, with only 319 rows from 13 districts (0.9 million hectares against 194.9 million the year before), so the series stops at 2019–20. That gives 23 usable years.

Seasons. Rows marked Whole Year are treated as their own crop-season lines. They overlap a named season for the same crop, district and year in 0.64 percent of cases, and only 34 of those 441 come within 2 percent of the seasonal sum, so summing seasons does not count the same land twice. Total area by year runs from 165 to 195 million hectares, at or below the published national gross cropped area of about 195 to 200 million.

Formulas. pi is crop i's share of the year's gross cropped area. D0 is the number of crops with positive area. D1 = exp(−Σ pi ln pi). D2 = 1/Σ pi2. Evenness = D1/D0. Each is computed for every district-year (14,089 observations) and averaged over the years a district has. D0 ≥ D1 ≥ D2 held in every observation.

Sample. Means for the 606-district analysis sample are 21.2 for the count of crops, 4.9 for the effective number of crops, 3.5 for the number of main crops and 0.254 for evenness. Means for all 725 districts with a crop record are 20.4, 4.71, 3.33 and 0.264. A count taken across all years is a median 1.50 times the yearly average over the 725 districts, and 172 of them have fewer than 20 years of data. Over all 755 districts in the crop statistics the figures are 1.49 and 205.

Largest crop. For each district and year, every crop's share of gross cropped area, with seasons summed. Each crop's share is averaged over the years, counting zero in years it is absent, and the largest crop is the one with the highest average share. The classes apply in order: near-monoculture when the largest crop holds at least two thirds, two crops hold the year when the two largest hold at least 80 percent, few crops when a district grows fewer than 10 crops a year, and mixed otherwise. The change over time compares 555 districts observed in at least five of the seven years in each of 1998–99 to 2004–05 and 2013–14 to 2019–20, with a Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the largest crop's share.

Seasons. The monsoon season groups the Kharif, Autumn and Winter season records, and rabi and summer are read as recorded. Whole Year records count in the annual measures and lead no season. Each district-year gets a pattern and a district takes its most frequent one. In 83 of the 725 districts that pattern holds in fewer than half the years. Where Whole Year records hold at least half the land the seasons cannot be read, which is the case for 26 of the 606 districts, 13 of them in Kerala and 6 in Tamil Nadu. Autumn and winter rice, the aus and aman crops of the east, fall in the same season, so a district growing both shows one monsoon rice crop. Tamil Nadu records rice under a single season until 2017–18, so Cauvery delta districts such as Thanjavur and Nagapattinam appear with one rice season.

Composite. The mean of min-max rescaled Shannon index, Simpson index and count of crops, each first averaged over a district's years. It was recomputed on 200 random draws of 80 percent of districts with a fixed seed, and each draw's figure is the mean absolute shift in score across the districts drawn. The composite appears here only as a comparison.

Reference. Jost, L. (2007), Partitioning diversity into independent alpha and beta components, Ecology 88(10), on effective numbers. doi 10.1890/06-1736.1.
