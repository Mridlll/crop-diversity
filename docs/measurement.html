Measurement

How crop diversity is measured

A typical district grows twenty-odd crops in a year and gives most of its land to two or three of them.

01 · Counting crops two ways

A district with twenty crops and four fifths of its land under rice has an effective number of crops below three.

Each plot is one percent of the district's gross cropped area, which counts a field twice if it carries two crops in a year. Effective number of crops = exp(Shannon index of land shares). Presets are invented, except the two named districts, which use their 2019–20 shares. Crops under half a percent of the land may get no plot of their own and still count in the measures.

The count of crops is how many different crops a district grows in a year, each counted once whatever its area. The effective number of crops weighs each crop by its share of gross cropped area, the land under crops in a year, in which a field sown in both the monsoon and the winter season counts twice. It equals the number of equally sized crops that would spread the land the same way. When every crop has the same share the two measures agree, and the effective number falls as any one crop takes more of the land.

Twenty crops with rice on 80 percent of the land give an effective number of crops of 2.97 at most, and less if the other nineteen are uneven. Bankura in West Bengal grew 23 crops in 2019–20, yet with rice on 78 percent of its land its effective number of crops was 2.4. The average district grows 21.4 crops in a year and has an effective number of 4.9.

Evenness is the effective number of crops divided by the count. It averages 0.250, so the typical district spreads its land about as evenly as a quarter of its crops sharing it equally would.

02 · Three weightings

The count of crops is always at least the effective number of crops, and the effective number is always at least the number of main crops.

D(q) = (Σ piq)1/(1−q), where pi is crop i's share of the land, with D1 taken as the limit exp(−Σ p ln p). The faint line is the previous example.

All three measures come from one formula with a single setting, called q, that controls how much the largest crops count. At q = 0 every crop counts once, whatever its area, which gives the count of crops, D0. At q = 1 each crop counts in proportion to its land, which gives the effective number of crops, D1. At q = 2 the dominant crops govern the result, which gives the number of main crops, D2, in which small crops barely register.

All three are in units of crops, so they can be read side by side. A doubling of the effective number of crops means diversity doubled. The Shannon index itself is measured in logarithms, so a doubling of it has no such reading. The curve can only fall or stay flat as q rises, so a district's effective number of crops is never above its count of crops.

03 · Reading the scale

The middle 80 percent of districts have an effective number of crops between 2.4 and 8.2.

Each dot is a district at the average of its yearly values. The shaded band holds the middle 80 percent of districts. The marks at 1 and 2 stand for one crop on all the land and two crops on equal land. Lowest and highest are the districts at each end of the measure shown. Hover or tap a dot or a mark.

On the effective number of crops, 1 means a single crop on all of a district's land and 2 means two crops on equal halves. Although no district in the sample comes down to 1, Bhadrak in Odisha gets close at 1.27, with rice on 96 percent of its land in an average year. The bottom decile sits below 2.4, in the band marked low.

The median district has 4.4. The average is higher at 4.9 because a smaller group of districts reaches far above the rest, and the top decile starts at 8.2. Chamarajanagara in Karnataka is the highest at 14.3.

The count of crops runs along a longer stretch of the same axis. The median district grows 22.7 crops in a year while the bottom decile grows fewer than 11.1. Kargil in Ladakh has the shortest list at 3.9 crops a year, and at the far end Belagavi in Karnataka grows 37.6. The number of main crops is the lowest of the three, running from 1.09 in Bhadrak to 10.9 in Chamarajanagara with a median of 3.0.

04 · Across districts

Districts with long crop lists do not reliably spread their land more evenly.

One dot per district, the average of its yearly values. White ticks are medians. Hover, tap or search for a district to follow it across the strips.

In most districts a long list of crops sits on land held mostly by a few of them. The gap between the count and the effective number is made of crops grown on areas too small to change how the district's land is spread.

The effective number of crops and the number of main crops rise and fall together across districts, with a correlation of 0.97. The count of crops moves much more on its own, with a correlation of 0.50 with the effective number. Evenness is lower where more crops are grown, with a correlation of −0.38 with the count, because added crops usually arrive on small plots.

05 · Monoculture

In 120 of the 606 districts one crop holds at least two thirds of the land, and in 117 of them that crop is rice.

Top: invented districts, fifty plots per season, each plot an equal piece of land. Empty plots are fallow. Below: the largest crop's share is its average share of gross cropped area over the years a district reports, so land cropped in two seasons counts twice. Near-monoculture means the largest crop holds at least two thirds. Two crops hold the year when the two largest hold at least 80 percent and neither reaches two thirds. Few crops means fewer than 10 a year.

A district counts as near-monoculture when its largest crop holds at least two thirds of its cropped land in an average year. That describes 120 districts, and rice is the crop in 117 of them, mostly in Odisha, Assam, Jharkhand, Chhattisgarh and West Bengal. Even so, these districts still grow about 17 crops a year, while their median effective number of crops is 2.6.

Growing few crops is a separate condition. Only 25 districts grow fewer than 10 crops a year without one crop on two thirds of the land or two crops on 80 percent. Most of them lie in the hills of Arunachal Pradesh, Sikkim, Jammu and Kashmir and Himachal Pradesh, with four in Punjab, and their median effective number of crops is 3.3.

Another 91 districts give at least 80 percent of their land to two crops, mostly in Uttar Pradesh, Bihar and Punjab; wheat is the larger of the pair in 38 and rice in 37. Across all 606 districts the largest crop's share has a correlation of −0.80 with the effective number of crops but only −0.27 with the count of crops.

Between 1998–2004 and 2013–2019, near-monoculture districts went from 133 to 114. The median share of the largest crop moved from 0.48 to 0.46, a change that is not statistically significant (p = 0.11).

06 · Cropping systems

Districts that grow rice twice a year have about two main crops, the same as districts with one rice crop and little after it.

A typical year in three systems, as a share of the year's cropped land

Map: each district's most frequent pattern across the years it reports. The monsoon season covers kharif and the autumn and winter rice crops, and the second season is rabi and summer together. Strips are medians within each system. Hover or tap a district.

Each year a district's land is split by season. Rice then wheat means rice leads the monsoon season and wheat leads rabi, each on at least a fifth of the year's land. Rice twice a year means rice also leads rabi or summer on at least 10 percent of the land. A district takes the pattern it shows most often, and the median district shows that pattern in 86 percent of its years. Rice then wheat covers 116 of the 606 districts, in Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Punjab and Haryana. Rice twice a year covers 39, in West Bengal, Assam, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and Odisha.

When a district's seasons are added into one year, a second rice crop raises rice's share without adding a crop. Districts growing rice twice end up with rice on 69 percent of their land and 2.1 main crops, much like the 159 districts that grow rice in the monsoon and little afterwards, which have 2.0. Wheat after rice, by contrast, counts as a second crop, so rice then wheat districts reach 2.9 main crops even though the two crops together hold 80 percent of the land.

07 · Within a season

In any one season the typical district grows between 2.6 and 3.0 effective crops, against 4.9 over the whole year.

over the year within a season

District means within each cropping system, with two districts for scale. Sugarcane, coconut and other year-round crops can sit with the monsoon crops that share their fields, or count as a season of their own. Hover or tap a cropping system for its values.

Adding a district's seasons into one year counts wheat after rice as a second crop, even though the two never share a field at the same time. Taken one season at a time, the typical district grows between 2.6 and 3.0 effective crops. Between a third and two fifths of annual diversity, measured on the Shannon index, comes from districts growing different crops in different seasons.

Rice then wheat districts lose the most when seasons are separated. They average 4.2 effective crops over a year but 2.0 to 2.1 within a season. Ludhiana grows 2.3 effective crops over a year and only 1.1 to 1.2 in any one season, because rice fills its monsoon and wheat its rabi. In a comparison within state (state fixed effects), the change of crops between seasons makes up 9 percentage points more of their annual diversity than elsewhere, and ranked on diversity within a season these districts drop between 64 and 75 places on average.

Districts that grow rice twice barely move in the ranking, since a second rice crop never added a crop in the first place. Purba Bardhaman grows 2.1 effective crops over a year and between 1.3 and 1.5 in a season.

Where cropping intensity is higher, land spreads across more seasons, yet the crop mixes in those seasons are no more distinct from each other, and rabi carries fewer crops. The fall in diversity across the most irrigated districts therefore happens within seasons, while the change of crops between seasons holds up as irrigation rises.

08 · A rescaled score

A score rescaled between the sample's least and most diverse districts changes when other districts enter or leave the sample.

Toy: invented districts, scored on the effective number of crops rescaled from 0 to 1. Data: the composite averages the rescaled count of crops, Shannon and Simpson indices, recomputed with a random fifth of districts left out each time.

A common way to combine diversity measures rescales each one so the least diverse district in the sample scores zero and the most diverse scores one, then averages them. A district's score then depends on which other districts are in the sample. With a random fifth of districts left out and every district's crops unchanged, a district's composite score moves by 0.0032 on average and by 0.019 at most. Both are small next to the composite's standard deviation, the typical distance of a district's score from the average, which is 0.18, yet they show the score is not a property of the district alone.

The equal weights also have no stated basis. Since Shannon and Simpson belong to the same family and correlate at 0.943 here, a composite of the two with the count hands what is close to a single land-weighted measure two thirds of the weight. The effective numbers need no rescaling, because each is measured in crops and depends on the district alone.

09 · Two kinds of change

The count of crops and the effective number of crops respond to different changes in a district's land.

The composite change is rescaled against the range across the 606 districts. Market coefficients come from one comparison per facility, within state and with development measures held constant. A single score that mixes the count of crops with how evenly land is spread cannot separate a district that adds a twenty-first crop on a small plot from one that moves land among the crops it already grows. The two measures keep them apart. In the example a crop added on one percent of the land raises the count by one and the effective number of crops by 0.2. Moving 10 percent of the land from the main cereal to the next four crops raises the effective number by 1.1, with the count still at 20.

The market results follow this split. A district where every rural resident lives in a village with a weekly haat records 6.28 more crops than one where none does (p < 0.001), yet its effective number of crops is only 0.55 higher (p = 0.44). A p value is the chance of seeing a link at least this large if there were none, and below 0.05 is the usual bar. One standard deviation of the haat share is 15 percentage points of the rural population, which goes with about 0.9 more crops. Producer organisations go with more of both, +1.89 on the effective number (p = 0.017) and +3.38 on the count (p = 0.024).

10 · Small crops over time

The count of crops rose from 21.2 to 25.3 between 1998–99 and 2019–20, and all of the rise came from crops on less than 0.1 percent of a district's land.

Means weighted by cropped area, over districts that report in every year. A crop counts in a year only when it holds at least the chosen share of the district's cropped land. Hover or tap for a year's values.

Counting only crops that hold at least 0.1 percent of a district's land, the average district grew 14.4 crops in 1998–99 and the same in 2019–20. The crops below that line went from 6.8 to 11.0 per district, more than the whole rise.

The starting year carries much of it. In 1998–99 several states filed crop lists far shorter than usual, and Madhya Pradesh named 17 crops where it usually names 36. Measured from 1999–2000 the rise is 1.62 crops, and from 2002–03 only 0.44. Leaving out Madhya Pradesh, Andhra Pradesh, Chhattisgarh and Nagaland, whose 1998–99 lists had collapsed, cuts the rise to 1.69, and once Odisha and Karnataka, whose lists were also short that year, are left out as well, no rise remains.

Counts that need a crop to hold half a percent or one percent of the land fell between 1998–2004 and 2013–2019, by 0.8 and 0.6 crops. The effective number of crops was 5.6 at the start and 5.5 at the end, and its trend is not statistically significant once each state's swings in reporting are allowed for. Longer crop lists mostly mean fuller returns, while the spread of land across crops has hardly moved.

11 · Within a year

Counting every crop a district records across two decades gives about one and a half times the count in a typical year.

counted across all years counted each year, then averaged

Left: an invented district. Right: district means, grouped by years of data, for groups of at least five districts; dot size follows the number of districts.

A crop grown once in two decades adds as much to a count taken across the whole period as a crop grown every year. Counting that way gives about one and a half times the count in a typical year. It also rises with the number of years a district has data for, since each extra year is another chance to record a rare crop, and about a quarter of districts have fewer than twenty years of data. Counted within each year and then averaged, the count of crops is far less tied to how long a district has been recorded: its correlation with years of data falls from 0.51 to 0.27.

The choice changes rankings and results. Ranked by the count of crops, districts move a median of 76 places between the two ways of counting, and 276 of 724 move by more than 100. Those that move furthest mostly recorded many crops across the period while growing few in any one year; Ranchi recorded 40 crops in all yet grew 11.6 in a typical year. Counted each year, the count of crops peaks at moderate irrigation like the other measures (p = 0.0001 for the peak), whereas a count taken across all years shows no clear peak (p = 0.19).

How this was estimated

Source. District crop area, production and yield, 1998–99 to 2019–20, covering 54 crops, summed across seasons to gross cropped area per crop, district and year. The series stops at 2019–20 because only 13 districts report the year after.

Why every figure starts in 1998–99. The 1997–98 returns are broken. West Bengal files 50.8 million hectares of rabi niger seed, 28 percent of all area recorded in India that year, against under 8,000 hectares the year after. Odisha files 0.91 million hectares in all against 5.23 million the next year, Chhattisgarh's kharif area is 0.48 million hectares against 4.56 million, and twelve states file crop lists under three fifths of their usual length. That year is left out of every district average as well as every change over time.

Seasons. Rows marked Whole Year are treated as their own crop-season lines. They overlap a named season for the same crop, district and year in 0.64 percent of cases, and only 34 of those 441 come within 2 percent of the seasonal sum, so summing seasons does not count the same land twice. Total area by year runs from 165 to 195 million hectares, at or below the published national gross cropped area of about 195 to 200 million.

Formulas. pi is crop i's share of the year's gross cropped area. D0 is the number of crops with positive area. D1 = exp(−Σ pi ln pi). D2 = 1/Σ pi2. Evenness = D1/D0. Each is computed for every district-year (13,605 observations) and averaged over the years a district has. D0 ≥ D1 ≥ D2 held in every observation.

Minimum share of land. A crop's share is its area summed across seasons divided by the district's gross cropped area that year. The thresholded counts keep crops at or above 0.1, 0.5 or 1 percent. The national series weights districts by cropped area and uses the 500 districts present in every year. Uncertainty on changes groups districts by state, since states file the returns, and comes from a bootstrap over the 28 states. The falls of 0.8 and 0.6 crops have 95 percent intervals of −1.69 to −0.14 and −1.13 to −0.09.

Seasons within a year. The monsoon season pools kharif, autumn and winter records, and rabi and summer are kept apart. Annual Shannon entropy splits exactly into a land-weighted average of the entropy within each season and a remainder that comes from crops differing between seasons. The within-season effective number of crops is the exponential of the first part. Year-round crops either join the monsoon season or form a season of their own, and the two give the range quoted. Years where seasons cannot be read, or where year-round crops hold half the land, are left out, and a district needs five readable years. That removes Tamil Nadu, Kerala and one Andaman district and leaves 560 districts. Autumn and winter rice in Assam both fall in the monsoon season.

Sample. Means for the 606-district analysis sample are 21.4 for the count of crops, 4.9 for the effective number of crops, 3.5 for the number of main crops and 0.250 for evenness. Means for all 724 districts with a crop record are 20.6, 4.73, 3.34 and 0.260. A count taken across all years is a median 1.49 times the yearly average over the 724 districts, and 172 of them have fewer than 20 years of data.

Largest crop. For each district and year, every crop's share of gross cropped area, with seasons summed. Each crop's share is averaged over the years, counting zero in years it is absent, and the largest crop is the one with the highest average share. The classes apply in order: near-monoculture when the largest crop holds at least two thirds, two crops hold the year when the two largest hold at least 80 percent, few crops when a district grows fewer than 10 crops a year, and mixed otherwise. The change over time compares 555 districts observed in at least five of the seven years in each of 1998–99 to 2004–05 and 2013–14 to 2019–20, with a Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the largest crop's share.

Seasons. The monsoon season groups the Kharif, Autumn and Winter season records, and rabi and summer are read as recorded. Whole Year records count in the annual measures and lead no season. Each district-year gets a pattern and a district takes its most frequent one. In 70 of the 724 districts that pattern holds in fewer than half the years. Where Whole Year records hold at least half the land the seasons cannot be read, which is the case for 26 of the 606 districts, 13 of them in Kerala and 6 in Tamil Nadu. Autumn and winter rice, the aus and aman crops of the east, fall in the same season, so a district growing both shows one monsoon rice crop. Tamil Nadu records rice under a single season until 2017–18, so Cauvery delta districts such as Thanjavur and Nagapattinam appear with one rice season.

Composite. The mean of min-max rescaled Shannon index, Simpson index and count of crops, each first averaged over a district's years. It was recomputed on 200 random draws of 80 percent of districts with a fixed seed, and each draw's figure is the mean absolute shift in score across the districts drawn. The composite appears here only as a comparison.

Reference. Jost, L. (2007), Partitioning diversity into independent alpha and beta components, Ecology 88(10), on effective numbers. doi 10.1890/06-1736.1.
