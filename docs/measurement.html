Layer one

Counting crops, and weighting them

Diversity has a natural unit. Using it separates two things that a single score runs together, and the separation carries most of the analytical weight.

Ecology has spent several decades arguing about how to put a number on diversity, and the argument has largely settled. Agricultural economics tends not to have noticed. This page works through why that matters here, then documents four problems found in the original construction when the indices were rebuilt from the raw crop file.

The starting point was a composite. Shannon entropy, the Simpson index and crop richness were each rescaled to run from zero to one across the sample, and the three were averaged with equal weights. Composites of this shape are common and they are convenient, which is most of why they persist. The difficulty is that each of the three steps embeds an assumption.

The rescaling makes a district's score depend on other districts

Min-max normalisation subtracts the sample minimum and divides by the sample range. A district's position therefore depends on which other districts happen to be in the file. Add a few very concentrated districts at the bottom of the range and everyone else's score rises, without any of them having changed what they grow.

This is straightforward to demonstrate. Recomputing the composite on repeated random subsamples and comparing each district against its own full-sample value gives the distribution below.

Figure 1

The composite moves when other districts move

Two hundred random eighty percent subsamples. Each observation is one subsample's mean absolute deviation from the full-sample score.

The displacement is small in absolute terms, on an index whose standard deviation is roughly 0.15, so this is a conceptual objection more than a numerical one. It is still an odd property for a measurement to have.

Equal weights are a decision nobody argued for

There is no stated reason to weight richness, Shannon and Simpson at a third each. Any other weighting is equally defensible, which is another way of saying none of them is.

Two of the three components measure nearly the same thing

This is the substantive problem rather than the aesthetic one. Shannon and Simpson are not independent quantities. They are the same family evaluated at different sensitivities to rare categories, and across these districts they correlate at about 0.94. A composite that averages them alongside richness is therefore roughly two parts evenness to one part count, not three equal parts. The weighting that was never argued for is not even the weighting that was applied.

Consequence A single number that blends how many crops a district grows with how evenly it spreads them cannot distinguish a district that adds a twentieth crop on half a hectare from one that rebalances its existing land. Later on, that distinction turns out to separate two kinds of market infrastructure that otherwise look alike.

The alternative

Diversity as an effective number of crops

The standard resolution is to convert each index into the number of equally-common categories that would produce the observed value. These are the Hill numbers, indexed by an order q that sets how much weight rare categories receive. They share a single unit, so they can be compared with each other and read without a scale.

The three orders used throughout this site, plus the ratio between the first two.
IndexDefinitionSensitivity to rare crops
D0crop richnessmaximal: a crop on one hectare counts as much as the staple
D1exp(Shannon)each crop weighted by its area share
D21 / Σp²minimal: dominated by the common crops
evennessD1 / D0how evenly area is spread, with the count divided out

D1 is the one to hold onto. It asks: if every crop in this district had the same area, how many crops would there have to be to give the diversity we observe? A district growing twenty crops with four fifths of its land under paddy does not behave like a twenty-crop district. It behaves like one with three or four, and D1 says so.

Two properties make this family easier to work with. The ordering D0 ≥ D1 ≥ D2 holds by construction, so it doubles as a correctness check on the code, and it passed on every district-year here. And because the unit is crops, a doubling of D1 means diversity genuinely doubled, which is not true of Shannon entropy in log units.

Figure 2

Where the districts sit on each order

Distribution across districts. As q rises, rare crops stop counting and the distribution collapses toward the staples.

The distance between the D0 and D1 distributions is the part of Indian cropping that exists on paper more than in the field: crops grown, but on areas too small to shape what the district is doing.

Construction

Four problems found in the original indices

Separately from the choice of index, the original indices were checked against the raw area–production–yield file they were built from. One suspicion turned out to be unfounded and three problems were real.

Cleared: seasons are not double counted

In this data, the label Whole Year is sometimes a separate reporting line for perennials and sometimes a total that already contains the seasonal rows. If it were routinely the latter, summing across seasons would double count every hectare. It is not. Only a small fraction of one percent of Whole Year cells overlap with a named season for the same crop and district, and few of those look like genuine totals. Reconstructed national gross cropped area lands in the range published figures put it, which rules out systematic duplication.

The last year is not a year

The dataset is labelled as running to 2020–21 and the project described itself as covering twenty-four agricultural years. The final year contains a few hundred rows across thirteen districts, against nineteen thousand rows and nearly two hundred million hectares the year before. It is a partial extract rather than a year of data.

Figure 3

Coverage collapses in the final year

Gross cropped area implied by the file, by agricultural year.

The usable range is 1997–98 to 2019–20, twenty-three years. The animated timeline elsewhere on this site still ends on the incomplete frame.

Richness was pooled across the whole period

This is the one that mattered. The original code grouped by district without grouping by year, so the richness figure counted every crop a district recorded at any point across two decades, rather than the crops it grows in a year. Two things follow. The measure runs about half again as high as annual richness. More awkwardly, it rewards being observed for longer, since more years of data mean more opportunities to record a crop once.

Roughly a quarter of districts have fewer than twenty years of data. Pooled richness correlates with a district's number of observed years at about 0.55, against roughly 0.31 for annual richness. Since richness carried a third of the composite, part of the headline index was measuring survey coverage.

Duplicate rows were summed

A small number of exact duplicates on district, year, season and crop survive the cleaning the original code performs, concentrated in one oilseed in one state. A group-and-sum adds them together, inflating area for those cells. The effect is negligible nationally and it is still wrong, so the rebuild collapses them.

Consequences

What the corrections change

Rebuilding the indices year by year, dropping the incomplete final year and collapsing the duplicates leaves the broad picture in place. The corrected and original composites correlate at about 0.95, and the state that came top before still comes top. But the agreement is less reassuring at district level than the correlation suggests.

Figure 5

How far districts move when the construction is fixed

Absolute change in rank position out of roughly 725 districts.

The districts that move furthest are ones that recorded many crops across the period without growing many in any single year, so pooling had been crediting them with diversity they did not have in the field.

The more useful consequence is not the reshuffling but what it unlocks. On the original pooled construction, crop richness showed no relationship at all with irrigation. On the corrected annual measure it shows the same pattern as every other index. A construction problem had been concealing a real result rather than manufacturing a false one, which is the more fortunate of the two ways for this kind of thing to go.
