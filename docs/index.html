Crop diversity across Indian districts

Crop richness, the effective number of crops and evenness for 725 districts and 54 crops, 1997–98 to 2019–20.

Area and production: ISB India Data Portal Rural structure: SHRUG 2.1 Code and data

The average Indian district records about twenty crops in a year and spreads its land as though it grew about five. Counting the crops a district records and weighting them by the area each takes give different answers, and the two diverge by a factor of about four.

Take a district that reports twenty distinct crops in a season. If its land were split evenly across those twenty, the count would describe it well. In practice one cereal usually takes most of the area and the remaining nineteen split what is left, often on areas of well under a percent of the district's cropped land each. A plain count treats those two districts identically. Sipahijala in Tripura records twenty crops in an average year and puts 93 percent of its land under rice.

The measure used throughout is the effective number of crops: the exponential of Shannon entropy computed on area shares, the Hill number of order one. Take each crop's share of a district's cropped area and ask how many crops of identical size would give the same spread. A district where one crop takes 90 percent of the land scores close to one however many crops it lists, and the effective count can never rise above the number of crops grown. The measure is set out in full alongside crop richness and the inverse Simpson count.

Evenness is the effective number of crops divided by the count of crops grown. It runs from near zero to one, and one means every crop a district records takes the same area. The national mean is 0.26, so about a quarter of the recorded crop count survives the area weighting.

The inverse Simpson count squares the area shares before summing, so a crop on 1 percent of the land contributes a hundredth of what a crop on 10 percent contributes. It asks the same question as the effective number of crops and discounts rare crops harder, which is why it is always the smaller of the two.

Chhattisgarh appears in neither block. It records 31.7 crops in an average year, more than any other state, and holds an effective count of 3.2 on an evenness of 0.11, the lowest of the 29. A high crop count and a narrow cropping system sit together comfortably.

Punjab has the second-lowest crop count of any state at 9.1 and an evenness of 0.32, above the national mean. Its narrowness comes from the small number of crops grown, and its area is spread relatively evenly across them.

Effective crop diversity, district by district

District means over the available years. The three buttons switch between crops grown, the effective number of crops and evenness.

Grey districts carry no crop area in the source, which is mostly urban and metropolitan territory. The eastern rice belt and the northwestern wheat belt read narrow on the effective count even where the plain crop count does not.

What sits underneath that ranking is concentration in two cereals. Of the 725 districts, 350 are rice-dominant and another 147 wheat-dominant, so about seven in ten Indian districts have one of two crops as their largest single land use. No other crop leads in more than 36 districts.

A crop's area share is its area divided by the district's total cropped area in that year, summed across seasons, so land cropped twice counts twice. Shares are computed each year and averaged across the district's available years.

Change

Change between 1997–98 and 2019–20

The area-weighted national mean of the effective number of crops was 5.5 in 1998–99 and 5.4 in 2019–20. Districts are weighted by cropped area, so a large wheat district counts for more than a small hill district. The series opens at 3.8 in 1997–98, the first year of the record, and stays between 5.2 and 5.9 for every year after that.

The effective count holds near five while crops grown rises from 21.2 in 1998–99 to 24.9 in 2019–20, so the additional crops enter on small areas.

Of the 429 districts observed in every year, 264 ended with a lower effective count than they started with and 165 with a higher one. The median change is a fall of 0.19 crops, comparing each district's mean over 1998 to 2004 against its mean over 2013 to 2019.

Where this goes next

Accounting for the pattern

Two things are examined against district diversity: irrigation, and access to markets and inputs. They are estimated separately because a district's irrigation share and its market access are themselves correlated. Irrigation systems and market infrastructure sit where land, politics and existing agriculture already favoured them, so every estimate is a cross-sectional association within states.

Every estimate carries state fixed effects. Each state is allowed its own baseline level of diversity, so only differences between districts in the same state carry the estimate, and Punjab is never compared with Karnataka. Adding them cuts the estimated irrigation turning point from 0.51 of cropped area irrigated to 0.28, and lifts the share of variation explained from 0.07 to 0.53.

The 2011 census split many districts. 162 of the matched districts are pieces of a unit that was single for most of the period and carry near-identical records, 64 of them inside the estimation sample. Treating those pieces as independent observations would make the sample look larger than it is, so uncertainty is computed on the pre-2011 parent, which widens the intervals.

Every market and infrastructure variable is a village share: the fraction of a district's villages that have the facility, counting villages equally regardless of size. A district of many small villages and one large market town scores low even if most people live near a market.

Three district counts run through the work. 725 districts have a usable crop record, and the descriptive figures use all of them. 606 of those also match the rural-structure data and clear the coverage threshold, and every estimate uses that set. 429 report in every one of the 23 years, and the national series uses those.

Estimates Irrigation

The effective number of crops rises with irrigated share and then falls. Under state fixed effects the turning point sits at about 0.28 of cropped area irrigated. Canal, surface and groundwater irrigation are estimated separately.

 Estimates Markets and inputs

Regulated mandis, weekly haats, fertiliser shops and cold stores, each measured as the share of a district's villages with the facility. Weekly haats go with 7.3 more crops grown (p < 0.01) and 0.4 more effective crops (p = 0.63).

How these differ from the figures above

The diversity values on these four maps come from a first pass at the indices, which counted the crops a district recorded at any point across the period rather than the crops it grows in a year. That runs about ten crops higher per district than the annual measure used everywhere else on this site, and it rises with how long a district was observed. The geography is broadly the same and individual districts move, so read them for a district's crop composition and caloric output rather than for its diversity value. Districts with only a year or two of record are drawn the same way as districts with twenty-three.
