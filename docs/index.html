District-level analysis · 1997–98 to 2019–20

What India grows, and why the mix looks the way it does

Crop diversity across roughly 725 districts and 54 crops: what the pattern is, and what it is arranged around.

Area and production: ISB India Data Portal Rural structure: SHRUG 2.1 Code and data

Indian districts grow a great many crops and farm very few of them. That sentence is not a paradox, and unpacking it is most of what follows. The distinction between crops that appear in a district's records and crops that shape what the district actually does turns out to matter for almost every question downstream of it.

Take a district that reports twenty distinct crops in a season. If its land were spread evenly, we would call it a twenty-crop district without hesitation. In practice one cereal usually takes most of the area and the remaining nineteen split what is left, often on plots small enough to be closer to kitchen gardening than to a cropping system. Counting crops describes the first district. It does not describe the second.

The measure used throughout is the effective number of crops: how many equally-sized crops it would take to produce the diversity actually observed. A district with twenty crops and one dominant cereal has an effective count of three or four, and that number describes how it behaves. The measure is set out in full alongside the two companion indices that sit either side of it.

Figure 1

Crops grown, against crops that count

Each point is a district averaged over its available years. The diagonal is what perfectly even cropping would look like.

No district comes close to the diagonal, and the vertical distance from it is the quantity of interest. Both axes are counts of crops, so the gap can be read directly rather than through a scale.

The national averages give the scale of that gap. Across districts the mean count of crops grown in a year sits around twenty, while the effective count sits near five and the count weighted toward dominant crops nearer three. Indian agriculture is, in the aggregate, a three-to-five crop system carrying a long tail.

Geography

Where the mix is wide and where it is narrow

Ranking states on the effective count produces a geography that will be familiar to anyone who knows Indian agriculture. The peninsular and central states run wide. The intensively irrigated northwest and the rice-dominated east run narrow.

The contrast between the two ends is not a story about how many crops exist in a state. It is a story about how much land the leading crop takes.

Effective crop diversity, district by district

Switch the measure to see how counting crops and weighting them disagree about which parts of the country are diverse.

Grey districts have no agricultural record here, which is mostly urban territory. The eastern rice belt and the northwestern wheat belt read narrow on the effective count even where the plain crop count does not.

What sits underneath that ranking is concentration in two cereals. Of the districts in the file, roughly half are rice-dominant and another fifth wheat-dominant, so about seven in ten Indian districts have one of two crops as their largest single land use. Everything else in the table below is a regional specialisation rather than a national pattern.

The composition of land use across diversity quartiles fills that picture in. Districts in the least diverse quarter are overwhelmingly cereal. As the effective count rises, cereal area gives way, and what replaces it is mostly oilseeds and pulses rather than horticulture. Diversification in the Indian context has largely meant a wider field-crop rotation, not a move into fruit and vegetables.

Change

Has any of this been moving?

Less than one might expect, and the answer depends on whether the question is about the country or about districts. Weighting districts by cropped area, so that a large wheat district counts for more than a small hill district, the national series sits close to flat across two decades. Whatever has been reshaping Indian agriculture over this period, it has not moved the aggregate crop mix much.

The panel is restricted to districts reporting in every year, so the series cannot be moved by districts entering or leaving the record.

A flat national aggregate is compatible with a good deal of district-level movement, and there is some. Comparing each district's early years against its late years, rather more districts lost effective diversity than gained it. The aggregate holds still because those losses fall in districts carrying less of the country's cropped area, which is worth knowing: the places narrowing are not the places that dominate the national totals.

Figure 7

District-level change in the effective number of crops

Mean over 1998–2004 against mean over 2013–2019, for districts observed throughout.

The distribution sits slightly left of zero. This is a real but modest drift, and it is worth being careful not to describe it as a collapse.

Where this goes next

Accounting for the pattern

With the pattern described, the rest takes it apart through two lenses. Each asks what a district's position is arranged around, and they are kept separate because the answers do not reduce to one another.

A caution that applies to all of them. None of these lenses is an experiment. Irrigation systems and market infrastructure are placed where the land, the politics and the existing agriculture already favoured them, so what follows is a description of how the cross-section hangs together. Where a hypothesis was set out in advance and then failed, the failure appears alongside the results that held.

Lens one Irrigation

The variable most often blamed for Indian monoculture. The relationship is not monotonic, the turning point depends on the index, and the source of the water carries information that the quantity does not.

 Lens two Markets and inputs

Regulated mandis, weekly haats, fertiliser shops and cold stores. One of these behaves unlike the others, and separating crop counts from crop balance changes what that difference means.
